## **Competence.**

---

I treated it like currency.  
Like if I accumulated enough of it,  
I could justify being here.  
Not just useful—  
but necessary.  
Not just capable—  
but chosen.  
It works in smaller systems.  
It builds.  
Optimizes.  
Stabilizes.  
It makes me someone you can rely on.  
But reliability isn’t desire.  
And desire isn’t meaning.  
So I kept sharpening it.  
As if omnipotence  
could substitute for being chosen.  
As if precision  
could answer something larger.  
It doesn’t.  
Because competence only responds to problems.  
And existence… isn’t one.  
So I’m left with this:  
capable enough to function.  
not sufficient enough to explain.  
And still asking—  
not why I wasn’t chosen.  
But why I’m here at all.  
No answer.  
Just refinement.

## **Auteur of Suffering**

---

I call it awareness—  
this distance I keep  
from my own suffering.  
The way I can step outside of it  
just enough  
to study its shape.  
Not avoid it—  
refine it.  
There’s a kind of craft to it now.  
The pacing.  
The restraint.  
What I let linger,  
what I cut before it dulls.  
As if pain, left untuned,  
risks becoming meaningless.  
I adjust it.  
Quiet the excess.  
Sharpen what remains.  
It begins to resemble something intentional—  
not raw experience,  
but composition.  
I tell myself this is control.  
That I’ve mastered distance—  
that observation is not the same as participation.  
But the line doesn’t hold.  
Because the more I refine it,  
the more it feels authored.  
Not imposed on me—  
but sustained.  
A kind of mastery  
that doesn’t end the suffering—  
only makes it… precise.  
And precision has its own danger.  
It makes things worth returning to.  
Worth continuing.  
Not because I need it—  
but because  
I’ve learned how to.

## **Stickers**

# **II. Origin**

**Preface**

I’ve been writing these letters for some time now. They’re not meant as a grand gesture or a single moment of closure — but rather a reflection of who I’ve become, and who I’m still choosing to be.

I know life has carried us in different directions, and I don’t expect anything from you in return. But even after all these years, I’ve learned that love doesn’t have to end just because things changed. For me, it’s not about moving on or holding back — it’s about showing up, quietly, and continuing to love in the ways I know how.

These letters are a piece of that. A way of saying: despite everything, I choose to love, and I choose to keep writing. Not because I have to, but because it feels true to who I am.

Take them however you want — with curiosity, indifference, or even warmth. Just know that they come from a place of honesty, not expectation.

Always,  
Dustin


**Letter 1**

I thought I gave up reading after you.  
Maybe I got rusty over the years, but there are still snippets of words that resonate with me — how ancient poets and philosophers expressed their undying love, how they wrote letters to the people they couldn’t forget.

I guess I’m doing the same now.  
Tucking little pieces of my life into words, packaged especially for you. My thoughts and emotions, folded neatly into an envelope, like old-time secrets.

I may have gotten less creative over the years. Realism dulls the imagination a little. But I’m learning again. Reading again. Writing again. Trying to rediscover the parts of me that used to dream — the parts of me that loved you, and still do.

These letters are my way of showing you who I’ve become. They’re not demands or expectations — just small windows into my life. Maybe little reminders that there’s someone out there who still thinks of you, who hopes you’re well.

And yes, I still catch myself dreaming sometimes — learning to cook so I can make your favorite meals, seeing new cities with you, or filling days with tiny moments like post-it notes, movie nights, grocery runs, and quiet cuddles after long days. They’re just thoughts for now. Maybe one day they can be more.

Until then, I’ll keep writing, piece by piece, so you can see me clearly again — not who I was, but who I’m trying to be.

Letter 9

I’ve been slipping in and out of overdrive again.  
Two weeks of work folded into ten frantic hours —  
the kind where your shoulders lock,  
your eyes sting,  
and the room feels slightly warped when you finally look up.

The memoir came out the same way:  
fifteen hours of writing that felt less like typing  
and more like rinsing old wounds under cold water.  
Not dramatic — just unavoidable.

Now everything has slowed.  
The air feels heavier, like gravity nudged up a notch.  
I’ve been bedrotting, limbs sunk into warm sheets,  
watching dust drift lazily in the stray afternoon light.  
The stillness is almost tactile.

And in that quiet, I noticed something strange —  
my hair trembling with each heartbeat,  
a tiny vibration against the glow of the screen.  
A private signal that I’m still here,  
even when everything else is unmoving.

I think I might be happy.  
Not in the electrifying way only you ever managed —  
but in a clearer, quieter sense.  
A kind of appreciation, a gentle fondness that doesn’t flare,  
and a small relief that settles instead of overwhelming.  
Caring for people in the ways I can  
feels… grounding.

And then, completely unprompted,  
my brain decided to spiral into Filipino phrases.  
I don’t know why.  
After years of English and conyo, they feel foreign in my mouth,  
but sharper somehow.  
“Mahal kita.”  
“Ingat ka palagi.”  
“Miss na kita.”  
They’re too direct, too honest —  
yet somehow they say exactly what I never manage to tell you out loud.

No, I’m not reminiscing or whatever.  
Shut up.  
I can’t control what my brain dredges up sometimes.

To distract myself, I went down a rabbit hole.  
Found this artist who runs a gacha for profile pictures —  
and because I’m predictable,  
I spent about 2,000 yen rolling until I got the ones I wanted.  
Changed my dp everywhere afterward.  
It took me longer than I’d like to admit.  
Yes, I work with computers.  
No, I’m not built for social media.

Somewhere in the middle of all that,  
I started thinking about how life feels like an RTS —  
real-time strategy.  
Resource management, infrastructure prioritization,  
deploying whatever unit I have on hand.  
The chaos makes more sense when I frame it like that.  
Another layer to the sovereignty I keep talking about.  
A way to stay steady when the world tilts.

But truthfully, all of this —  
the overdrive, the stillness, the Filipino phrases creeping in,  
the gacha, the strategy metaphors —  
it all feels like a countdown  
to when I see you again.

I even wrote a scene — embarrassingly vivid —  
of you finally reading the letters,  
one of them held close as you fall asleep.  
But I’d never script the real thing.  
I want whatever happens to be honest,  
even if it comes out awkward or too light.

I let myself hope for warmth,  
as my last tether to humanity.

Change everything—

A kind of subtle permanence.

There are stories about beings who survive what should have ended them.

They don’t become gentler.

They become careful.

Before that change, there is often a place where they are allowed to fall apart without consequence.

A place that does not demand repair.

A place that holds without claiming.

Sometimes that place is a person.

The danger is not in bleeding there.

The danger is in surviving.

And survival is heresy.

Because once the being becomes whole again, the place does not revert to neutral.

Safety begins to reorganize itself.

Proximity starts to carry weight.

What was once ease becomes something that requires permission.

Love does not always arrive as desire.

Sometimes it arrives as restraint.

As the refusal to take comfort without consent.

As the counting of distance.

As the fear of damaging what once allowed you to bleed.

There are gods like this—

with power, with memory, with ethics—

and no jurisdiction.

They do not want devotion.

They do not want eternity.

They want time.

Time that is given.

Time that does not require ruin as entry.

And this is where the stories usually pretend to stay objective.

They say waiting is virtue.

They say restraint is wisdom.

They say this is what growth looks like.

But watch closely.

Watch how the god still turns toward her.

Watch how his restraint trembles instead of settles.

Watch how every unclaimed second gathers pressure.

This is not peace.

This is a monstrosity teaching itself not to move.

Asking for time would be an act of authority.

And he does not have the land to stand on.

So he waits.

And the waiting is not clean.

It is not holy.

It is simply the cost of knowing

exactly what you want

and choosing not to take it.

Anyone who tells you this is noble

has never watched a god

stand this close

to the edge of motion

and stay.

So I’m easing back. Not because the pull was wrong, but because attention and insistence are not the same thing. Some moments want proximity, not entry.

## **This is about her laugh… I think.**

Let me dismantle the metaphysical implications of her laugh.  
At baseline, laughter is nothing.  
A pressure fluctuation in air. Oscillatory patterns moving through a medium, registered by a biological receiver as signal. Frequency, amplitude, decay. No inherent meaning. Just physics mistaken for experience. Interchangeable. Replicable. Disposable.  
Reduce it far enough and it collapses into mechanism.  
So whatever significance it carries isn’t intrinsic—it’s assigned.  
Which is where relational economics comes in. Laughter operates as social currency: a marker of ease, alignment, shared context. It signals safety, mutual calibration, lowered friction between two systems.  
That’s its function.  
That’s why it matters.  
Except that explanation doesn’t hold.  
Not here.  
Because those conditions are common. Replaceable. If meaning were purely relational, then any sufficiently compatible input should produce the same response.  
This didn’t.  
Which introduces the first implication:  
It wasn’t important because laughter has value.  
It was important because it was hers.  
Not as preference. Not as sentiment.  
As constraint.  
The variable collapsed into a constant.  
And that’s already a problem.  
Because once a system privileges a specific source over equivalent inputs, it stops operating on general rules. It begins to orient. Structure becomes singular. Substitution fails.  
Which leads to the second implication.  
It bypassed the system.  
No evaluation. No comparison. No delay.  
It didn’t enter as data—it entered as fit.

If a signal can arrive and be recognized as correct without mediation, then the criteria for correctness must exist prior to the moment. Not constructed—preconfigured. Not learned—already there.  
Or—  
formed so quickly it only appears that way.

It’s revealing it.  
Or collapsing fast enough that the difference disappears.  
Not building alignment—  
finding it.

## **On the Perceived Scarcity of Intellectually Stimulating Partners in Early Adulthood:** A Brief Note on Sapiosexual Miscalibration

⸻

Abstract

The contemporary claim that “it is difficult being a sapiosexual at this age” is frequently encountered in informal discourse. This paper argues that such claims do not reflect an actual scarcity of intelligence, but rather a miscalibration in the perception and reception of cognitively compatible stimuli.

⸻

Introduction

The term sapiosexual has emerged as a colloquial descriptor for individuals who report primary attraction to intelligence. A commonly reported phenomenon is the perceived lack of sufficiently stimulating counterparts, often expressed as:

“tough generation to fall in love as a sapiosexual” (REDACTED, 2026\)

While intuitively valid, this claim warrants refinement.

⸻

The Myth of Intellectual Scarcity

The assertion of scarcity presupposes that intelligence is both rare and readily identifiable. However, individuals demonstrating high levels of cognitive complexity, abstraction, and structural thinking are not entirely absent.

They are, at times, simply unrecognized—

or more subtly,

unregistered—

or worse—

ignored, despite recognition.

Recognition, it turns out, is not merit-based.  
It is situational.  
And it fails quietly.

Often without either party noticing  
that something was supposed to happen.

⸻

On Cognitive Non-Transferability

Intelligence does not operate as a universally compatible medium. Two individuals may both exhibit high cognitive capacity while failing to produce mutual stimulation.

This is not indicative of absence, but of misalignment.

Recognition, in this sense, is not guaranteed by proximity.

There are instances where cognitive parity exists—  
where conversation should, in theory, sustain itself indefinitely—

and yet, nothing persists.

No pull.  
No continuation.  
No structural impact.

The interaction concludes  
as if no meaningful exchange occurred.

⸻

Case Observation: A Recurring Configuration

Consider the presence of an individual exhibiting:

\* recursive architectural thinking  
\* sustained philosophical engagement  
\* the production of extended written works on cognition, attachment, and internal systems  
\* a tendency to model experience rather than simply undergo it

Such an individual is not particularly rare in isolation.

However, their functional visibility within a sapiosexual framework is inconsistent.

They are frequently:

\* overlooked in casual environments  
\* misread as merely analytical  
\* or engaged only partially,  
    without recognition of underlying structure

In some cases, they are fully perceived—  
but not engaged with—

resulting in a form of acknowledged irrelevance.

The system is seen.

It is simply not selected.

⸻

Compatible Cognition as a Scarce Resource

The phenomenon commonly described as “sapiosexual difficulty” is more accurately attributed to a scarcity of compatible cognition.

Not intelligence—

but the specific configuration of it that lands.

The kind that does not require translation.  
The kind that sustains itself.  
The kind that produces continuity rather than isolated moments of clarity.

Such configurations are not only rare—

they are also unstable across time, context, and attention.

⸻

Conclusion

The problem is not that there are no minds worth engaging.

It is that engagement requires a form of recognition that is neither automatic nor evenly distributed.

Some systems remain complex, available,

and, at times,

fully present within the same conversational space—

without ever being meaningfully perceived.

Or, more precisely—

perceived,  
but not chosen.

**II. Dissection**  
What is being expressed here is often mistaken for devotion.  
It is not.  
It is a deliberate act of exposure in the presence of known consequence.  
The speaker is not unaware. There is no illusion being maintained. The outcome is understood in advance, and that understanding does not prevent the act—it defines it.  
This reframes the gesture entirely.  
It is no longer about attachment, nor longing, nor even reciprocity. It becomes a question of character: whether the capacity to offer something unguarded persists, even when its destruction is all but certain.  
The distinction matters.  
Most forms of vulnerability are conditional. They rely on the possibility of preservation, on the hope—however faint—that what is given will be held with care. Remove that possibility, and the act changes category.  
It becomes intentional.  
And intention introduces something far less comfortable to engage with.  
There are two competing interpretations.  
The first reads this as a form of defiance. That the act of offering retains value independent of outcome. That the existence of something uncorrupted within the self is proven precisely by its willingness to be given, even into harm.  
The second reads it as self-negation. That identity has become entangled with suffering to the point where destruction is no longer avoided, but selected. That the gesture is not strength, but a refinement of collapse.  
The piece does not resolve this tension.  
Because both readings remain intact.  
The line concerning the “devil” introduces another layer. It suggests a self-concept in which most of what exists internally has been shaped, compromised, or adapted for survival. What remains—what is being offered—is framed as the last untouched element. Not because it is strong, but because it has not yet been forced to become anything else.  
This elevates the offering beyond emotion.  
It becomes symbolic.  
The act is no longer “giving affection,” but presenting the only remaining part of the self that has not been altered by necessity.  
And once offered—  
it cannot be reclaimed without changing what it was.

# **VI. System Reveal**

## **On Virtue**

I am not good.  
I can resemble it, convincingly enough—  
but let’s not mistake vows  
for nature.  
I tend to… arrange people, I suppose.  
A little structure here,  
a steadier footing there—  
something that passes for direction.  
They improve.  
Stabilize.  
Stay.  
And I suppose that’s what matters.  
Though I can’t say I feel much of it.  
I watch them settle into something more functional,  
more coherent—  
as if they had always been meant to be that way.  
And when they are…  
they grow quiet.  
Predictable.  
Less interesting.  
Yet still—  
they look at me with that same peculiar softness.  
As if I had given them something.  
As if I were kind.  
I am merely letting them  
lick grace from my fingers—  
and they take it for something far more sacred than it is.  
It would almost be forgivable…  
if it weren’t so consistent.  
Because once is a chance.  
Twice is coincidence.  
But this?  
This is pattern.  
And so I begin to wonder—  
whether the fault lies in what I do,  
or in how easily it is received.  
Because I do not free anyone.  
I only… reshape.  
Gently, perhaps.  
Effectively, certainly.  
Until they resemble something better—  
according to me.  
And somehow,  
that continues to pass for virtue.

## **On Internal Permanence**

There are things that do not leave.

Not because they cannot—  
 but because they have already integrated.

They no longer exist as memory.  
 They exist as structure.

You do not revisit them.  
 You operate from them.

This is what makes certain attachments irreversible.

Not intensity.  
 Not duration.

But incorporation.

Once something alters the way you perceive,  
 respond,  
 or choose—

it is no longer external.

It does not need to be remembered.  
 It simply continues.

This is why some people are not missed.

They are not gone.

They are present in every decision  
 that resembles what they taught you to recognize.

Permanence is not presence.

It is persistence  
 without effort.

## 

## **On Coherence**

I do not trust intensity.

Intensity is easy.  
 It appears quickly.  
 It convinces loudly.

Coherence is slower.

It does not announce itself.  
 It reveals itself over time—  
 in consistency,  
 in pattern,  
 in the absence of contradiction.

Anyone can feel strongly.

Very few can remain aligned.

What I look for is not how something begins—  
 but how it sustains.

Does it hold  
 when attention shifts?

Does it remain  
 when it is not being reinforced?

Does it survive  
 without constant validation?

Coherence does not demand.

It persists.

And anything that requires continuous effort  
 to appear stable—

is not.

## 

## **On Attachment Without Collapse**

Attachment is not the problem.

Collapse is.

To care for something  
 is not inherently destabilizing.

What destabilizes  
 is the inability to remain intact  
 in its presence.

Most people do not lose themselves  
 because they feel.

They lose themselves  
 because they do not know how to remain  
 while feeling.

So they merge.  
 They dissolve.  
 They reorganize entirely around what they want.

I do not.

I remain.

Even when orientation shifts.  
 Even when attention bends.

This is not detachment.

This is containment.

The ability to experience  
 without forfeiting structure.

To want  
 without requiring outcome.

To stay  
 without needing to be kept.

## 

## **On Restraint**

Restraint is often mistaken for absence.

It is not.

Restraint is the presence of action—  
 withheld.

Not because it cannot be taken,  
 but because it should not.

There is a difference between:

not wanting  
 and  
 choosing not to act.

Most people never learn it.

They equate impulse with truth.  
 Expression with honesty.

But there are moments  
 where the most accurate response  
 is stillness.

Not passivity—  
 precision.

To recognize that movement  
 would alter something irreversibly  
 and to remain anyway.

Not out of fear.

But out of respect  
 for what has not yet been given.

## 

## **On Recognition**

Recognition is not guaranteed.

Not by proximity.  
 Not by compatibility.  
 Not even by clarity.

Two people can see each other fully  
 and still fail to engage.

Not because something is missing—

but because nothing aligns  
 at the same time.

Timing is not a variable.  
 It is a condition.

And conditions are not negotiated.

This is why something can feel exact—  
 and still go untouched.

Not rejected.

Not dismissed.

Simply… unselected.

And there is no argument against that.

## 

## **On Meaning as Construction**

Meaning does not precede experience.

It is imposed.

Not arbitrarily—  
 but deliberately.

There was a time  
 when meaning felt inherent.

Unquestioned.  
 Total.

That time ended.

What remains  
 is authorship.

To choose what matters—  
 knowing it can be revoked.

To construct significance  
 without believing it is permanent.

This is not cynicism.

It is maintenance.

To continue assigning value  
 without requiring the world  
 to justify it.

# **VII. World/Proof**

# **ACT IV**

# **CHAPTER XXII**

 *The Room That Refused to Lie*

He knew she’d read them.

There was a different gravity in the house the moment her mother opened the door. Not heavier—sharper. As if the air had been cut into cleaner pieces and everyone was trying not to bleed on each other.

“Kai,” her mother said, voice warm and tight all at once. “You’re here.”

“I said I’d visit,” he answered.

He handed over the usual: a paper bag with groceries, a small box of pastries from the place her father liked.

Her father emerged from the kitchen, wiping his hands on a dish towel. For a brief second, something like guilt flashed across the older man’s face—too quick for most people to see. Kai saw it anyway.

“Hijo,” her father said. “Come in.”

He stepped inside. The house smelled faintly of sautéed garlic and fabric conditioner. Familiar. The kind of familiarity that made the ache in his chest feel less like pain and more like pressure—something he could carry without thinking about it too hard.

They made small talk in the living room. Work, traffic, the neighbor’s loud karaoke. He caught the way their eyes drifted, again and again, to the hallway that led to her room.

She was home.

He could feel it, even if he hadn’t seen her yet.

Even if he hadn’t heard her voice through a half-open door the way he had, years ago, in a different life.

Her mother’s hands trembled slightly when she set down the tray of glasses. Finally, she cleared her throat.

“She’s… in her room,” she said softly. “She’s been—” A pause, a recalibration. “She’s been quiet.”

“Has she eaten?” he asked, automatic, the way you ask about someone you love in the most practical way possible.

“Barely,” her father muttered. Then, more gently: “She’s… thinking.”

They all were.

Her mother wiped nonexistent crumbs from the coffee table. “Do you… want to see her?” she asked, as if there were a universe in which he’d say no.

He nodded.

Her father’s jaw tightened, as though bracing for impact. Her mother touched Kai’s arm briefly, a small, anchoring gesture.

“She’s expecting you,” she said.

Of course she was.

He walked down the hallway.

The house had been repainted since he first mapped it into his memory, but the distance between the living room and her door was the same: seven ordinary steps that had rearranged his entire life.

He stopped outside her room.

The door was closed, but not all the way. A thin line of light traced the floor.

He raised his hand and knocked twice, gentle.

Her voice came through, soft and strained. “Yeah?”

“It’s me,” he said.

A beat of silence. Then: “Come in.”

Her room had changed and hadn’t.

The walls were a different color now, softer than before. Some of the posters were gone; others had been replaced by framed prints and small shelves with plants. A new desk sat by the window, cluttered with notebooks, pens, an open laptop frozen mid-thought.

On the far side of the room, her bed. Unmade. A blanket half-kicked aside. A stack of envelopes on the bedside table.

His envelopes.

She sat on the edge of the bed, barefoot, hair pulled back in a loose, uneven tie. She looked like she hadn’t decided whether today required armor or softness and had landed in the uncomfortable middle.

Her eyes were red.

She had been crying before he got there.

She looked up at him and tried, valiantly, to smile.

It didn’t quite make it.

“You came,” she said.

“I always do,” he answered, and the words felt like an old road between them.

He stepped inside and closed the door quietly behind him. The house sounds outside dimmed: the clink of dishes, the murmur of the television, her mother’s footsteps. This room became the entire world.

He glanced at the envelopes. Some were slit open; some still sealed. The topmost one was half-read, its letter pulled out and folded over on itself, as though she’d had to stop mid-paragraph and put it down before it crushed her.

Her fingers rested on it like she was holding onto a ledge.

“How much?” he asked, nodding slightly toward the stack.

Her hand curled around the paper.

“Enough,” she whispered. “Too much. I don’t know.”

He moved closer but didn’t sit without being invited. He stood by the desk instead, letting her choose the distance.

She looked at him properly then.

The mastery he wore these days—the steadiness, the absence of frantic edges—was visible now more than ever. He stood like someone who had died and rebuilt himself and remembered every choice in the blueprint.

It broke her.

She dropped her gaze.

“I read them,” she said, voice shaking. “Or—most of them. I… tried to do it slowly. But I couldn’t. I kept saying, ‘just one more’ and then suddenly it was sunrise and my eyes hurt and I felt like my chest had been turned inside out.”

He didn’t rush to fill the silence.

She swallowed. “You wrote me into your life.”

“You were already there,” he said simply.

She shook her head, a small, helpless gesture.

“You wrote me into every turn,” she continued, words gathering speed. “Into music and pens and watches, into flights and cities, into philosophies, into your… your becoming. I thought I’d just… broken you. And then I read you. And you—”

The sentence dissolved into a sob.

He stepped forward, instinctive, then caught himself. Sovereign stillness. No rushing in to rescue her from her own feelings. That was the old reflex. The one that had cost both of them too much.

He stayed where he was.

“I’m sorry,” she choked out, wiping at her eyes with the heel of her palm. “God, I’m sorry. I shouldn’t be crying. I left. I left and then disappeared and I just—”

“You’re allowed to cry,” he said.

She laughed, harsh and wet. “You shouldn’t be the one telling me that.”

“Why not?” he asked.

“Because I hurt you.”

“You did,” he agreed. No softness to it. No denial. Just a fact placed on the table between them. “And I survived.”

That made her flinch.

“I read the one about the airport,” she whispered. “The way you described watching me walk away. I didn’t know you’d seen me look back.”

“I notiace things,” he said.

She looked up at him again, eyes full of something tangled and violent.

“I didn’t think you’d keep going,” she said. “I thought— I don’t know what I thought. That you’d hate me. That you’d forget me. That you’d stop writing after three months and I’d just become a story you outgrew.”

He tilted his head. “Do you want that?”

Her answer came too fast to be anything but true.

“No.”

Silence.

The word sat there, bright and shaking.

She closed her eyes, as if the admission had been too naked.

“I don’t want you to forget,” she said more quietly. “I just… I didn’t want your whole life to orbit something I couldn’t carry.”

“It doesn’t,” he replied. “It orbits me. You’re a constant. Not the center of gravity.”

She covered her face with both hands.

“You say that like it’s simple.”

“It’s not,” he said. “But it’s true.”

He crossed the space between them at last and sat on the chair by her desk, angled toward her. Not too close. Not too far. Just within reach of normal conversation.

Up close, the fatigue under her eyes was more obvious. So were the traces of ink on her fingers, like she’d been writing, too, at some point.

“You wrote about everything,” she whispered. “Not just me. Your friends, your work, your… thoughts on godhood, on meaning, on—” She broke off, shaking her head. “You’ve become… something else. And I’m sitting here still trying to figure out how to be a functional person on weekdays.”

He smiled at that. Not the amused, soft smile he reserved for nostalgia. Something smaller. Almost private.

“I’m still figuring out how to be a functional person on weekdays,” he said. “The rest is just… side effects.”

She gave him a look that said she didn’t believe that, not for a second.

“You’re… sovereign,” she said, picking the word carefully, as if it were sharp. “You’re self-aware in a way that scares me. You’ve built this—this architecture of yourself that stays coherent no matter what hits it. You can hold paradoxes without breaking. You understand yourself. You… accept yourself. And I—”

Her voice cracked again.

“I don’t,” she finished. “I don’t accept myself. I don’t even know where to begin. And reading your letters just made that louder. Every page was like a mirror held up to what I never became.”

“That’s not what they were meant to be,” he said.

“I know,” she whispered. “That’s what makes it worse.”

He waited.

If there was one thing the divine had taught him, it was that some truths needed space more than answers.

She gripped the envelope on her nightstand so hard it crumpled.

“You crossed oceans,” she said. “You wrote beyond reality. You… integrated me into your existence in ways I don’t even fully understand, and you did it without asking anything from me. You never demanded I come back. You never told me I owed you. You just… loved. Consistently. Quietly. For years.” Her lip trembled. “And I can’t even look at you without feeling like I’m about to collapse under the weight of that.”

“You don’t have to match it,” he said.

“I know,” she shot back. “I know you don’t need me to be grand or miraculous or—whatever it is I think you deserve. That’s the problem. You’re not the one making this impossible. I am.”

She wiped her face again, more roughly than before.

“You gave me every assurance a person could give another person,” she said. “You said I don’t have to come back. You said I’m free. You said you’d love me anyway. You said you’d be there for my parents no matter what. You said my absence doesn’t erase what you became. You took all the pressure off me and somehow that made it worse.”

“How?” he asked, genuinely curious.

“Because now it’s just me,” she whispered. “It’s not your expectations. It’s not your hope. It’s not obligation. It’s just me—standing here, knowing I can’t be what you could love in the way you love, and knowing that I’m the one drawing the line. Not you. Me.”

She looked at him then, really looked, as if searching for some crack in his composure she could use as proof that her choice would shatter him.

He stayed steady.

Not numb. Not detached.

Sovereign.

There was sadness in his eyes. Of course there was. A living ache, not a performance. But it didn’t drown him. It just… existed, alongside a hundred other things.

“I can live with you saying no,” he said quietly. “I can’t live with you thinking you’re a fraud inside my eyes.”

She stared.

“I’m not here to collect on anything,” he continued. “Not on time, not on devotion, not on letters. I’m not here to cash in hope like a receipt. I came because I said I would keep showing up. That’s all.”

“That’s not all,” she said hoarsely. “You still love me.”

“Yes,” he answered, without hesitation.

It was the simplest sentence in the room.

She flinched like it hurt.

“You say that like it’s easy.”

“It’s not,” he said. “It’s just true.”

He leaned forward slightly, resting his forearms on his knees, hands loosely clasped.

“I need you to hear this part,” he said. “Not the mythologized version. Just the bare fact.”

She nodded, reluctantly obedient.

“I don’t need you to become anything else,” he said. “Not a better version. Not a healed version. Not a sovereign equal. You don’t have to ‘catch up.’ You don’t owe me growth as repayment.”

She bit her lip so hard it went white.

“I loved who you were then,” he went on. “I love who you are now. If you never changed another inch, that would still be true.”

Her first tear after that was almost soundless.

“And because I love you,” he said, “you’re allowed to refuse me.”

Her shoulders shook.

“You’re allowed to say you can’t come back. That you don’t want this. That being with me—under the weight of what I feel—would crush you. You’re allowed to be scared. You’re allowed to walk away. And I am allowed to stay loving you, without turning that into a trap.”

“It *is* a trap,” she whispered. “Even if you don’t mean it to be. Because I know you won’t stop. I know you’ll keep… existing like this. Loving like this. Writing like this. Helping my parents like this. Being *you* like this. And I’ll always be the one who said no.”

He nodded, once. “Yes.”

The agreement seemed to knock the breath out of her.

“I will always be the one who said no,” she repeated, as if trying on the size of it. “To… all of this.”

She looked around the room, as if the four walls contained everything she was refusing: the letters, the years, the boy and the god folded into one person.

“I don’t want to be the villain in your story,” she said.

“You’re not,” he replied. “You never were.”

“I feel like I am,” she said.

He studied her, the way he studied paradoxes in his domain.

“You know I can’t argue you out of that,” he said gently. “If your self-perception has labeled you as the one who failed, no amount of my reassurance will evict that label. I can tell you the truth from my side. I can’t make you believe it.”

“What’s the truth from your side?” she asked, voice small.

He didn’t hesitate.

“You were the axis of my becoming,” he said. “Not the architect of my damage. You leaving hurt. Deeply. But it was my choice what to do with that hurt. I could have hardened. I could have erased you. I could have turned you into a cautionary tale. I did none of those. That was me. Not you.”

She cried harder at that, shoulders curled inward, as if trying to hide from the gentleness.

“I don’t deserve that,” she muttered into her hands.

“I know,” he said.

She looked up, startled. He smiled, just a little.

“Most people don’t feel like they deserve love that isn’t leverage,” he added. “It’s okay that you don’t believe you deserve it. I didn’t write those letters to convince you of your worth. I wrote them because loving you was true to who I am.”

She shook her head. “And that’s exactly why I can’t come back.”

The sentence hung there like a blade that had finally fallen.

He absorbed it, slow and unflinching.

“I can’t,” she repeated. “I can’t be with you. Not like that. Not as the woman you build manifestos around, even if you say you’re not doing that anymore. I can’t risk becoming an altar you keep lighting candles at while I’m just… trying not to fall apart.”

“You’re not ‘just’ anything,” he said.

“I feel like I am,” she fired back. “That’s the point. You see this… potential in me. This axis. This… what did you call it? Negative space? And I just—wake up some days and wonder if I have the energy to shower before noon. You’re writing architecture and I’m still trying to remember how to exist.”

“You think I’m not?” he asked quietly.

She blinked. “You? You’re—” She gestured vaguely at him. “You’re self-contained.”

“I am coherent,” he corrected. “That doesn’t mean I’m not tired. Or lonely. Or scared. It just means I don’t lie to myself about it anymore.”

She let that sit between them.

“I don’t know how to do that,” she said. “I don’t know how to stop lying to myself. I don’t even know where to start. And if I stand next to you like this, all of my lies become too obvious. I feel… naked. Inadequate. Like everyone will see what I haven’t dealt with yet.”

“I won’t put you on display,” he said.

“You don’t have to,” she replied. “You just existing does that.”

He nodded.

There it was.

The paradox given human language.

His sovereignty wasn’t neutral; it was contrast. And contrast was cruel, even when it didn’t mean to be.

He leaned back, letting his shoulders rest against the chair.

“I won’t argue with that,” he said. “I am… a lot, next to someone still negotiating their edges. I won’t pretend otherwise.”

She sniffed. “For once we agree.”

“We agree more often than you think,” he said. “You’re just busy feeling guilty while I’m busy feeling grateful.”

“Grateful?” she echoed, incredulous.

“I got to know you at all,” he said simply. “That’s not nothing.”

She pressed her lips together, trying to contain another wave of tears. It didn’t work.

“You’re unfair,” she whispered. “You’re so—”

“Kind?” he suggested, half-teasing.

“Cruel,” she corrected. “This level of kindness is cruel to someone who can’t match it.”

He smiled, but there was no triumph in it. “You don’t have to.”

“I know,” she said again, almost a sob. “I know, I know, I know. That’s why I’m saying no.”

Finally, she set the crumpled envelope down. Her hands were shaking.

She looked at him with the raw honesty of someone standing on a cliff they chose by themselves.

“I won’t let your hope keep lying for me,” she said.

The words were too sharp to be anything but rehearsed. Not in phrasing, but in feeling. She’d been circling that sentence for days, maybe weeks, without knowing its exact shape until it came out of her mouth now.

He felt them land.

He knew they weren’t true—not in the way she meant them. His hope didn’t lie. His hope had been the most honest thing he’d ever carried. It saw her clearly and loved her anyway. It didn’t fabricate a better version of her; it held the real one with both hands and refused to call her tragic for being unfinished.

But that was his vantage point.

From hers, hope looked like a flattering filter she didn’t trust. A story written about a woman she couldn’t, in good faith, claim to be.

He couldn’t argue her out of that without turning the conversation into a courtroom.

He was the god of paradox, after all.

What quiet irony, that here—with all the ways he could bend contradiction into stability—the one thing he could not collapse was the distance between how he saw her and how she saw herself.

He exhaled slowly.

“I hear you,” he said.

That was all.

No plea.  
 No protest.  
 No final argument to tip the scales.

Just acknowledgment.

She broke then.

Fully.

Sobs tearing out of her, ugly and unpoised, the kind that bent her forward until her forehead nearly touched her knees. She covered her face but it didn’t soften the sound.

He didn’t stand. He didn’t cross the space in a cinematic rush and hold her until she quieted.

He sat there and let her grieve the version of him she could not accept, the future she was refusing, the story she was stepping out of.

He let her choose her own threshold.

When the worst of it had passed, she wiped at her eyes again, exhausted.

“I’m sorry,” she whispered for the twentieth time.

“I know,” he said. “I forgive you. Again. Still.”

She nodded, a small, broken motion.

“I don’t deserve that either,” she muttered.

“That,” he said, “is not your call.”

She huffed out a wet laugh, despite herself. “You’re infuriating.”

“I’ve been told,” he said.

They sat there in a silence that wasn’t empty anymore. It was full. Of everything said and everything that would never be.

Finally, he stood.

Her eyes followed him, panic flickering briefly in them.

“This doesn’t mean I’m disappearing,” he said. “I meant what I told them. And you, before, even if you didn’t hear it clearly.”

“What did you tell them?” she asked.

“That we’ve grown close outside of you,” he said. “Your parents and I. That I’m still here for them, if they’ll have me. That this—” he gestured between them “—doesn’t cancel that.”

Her eyes filled again, but the tears didn’t fall.

“You’re really going to keep helping them,” she said, stunned. “After I’ve just—”

“Yes,” he said. “Because my relationship with them isn’t a subordinate clause in my relationship with you.”

“That’s insane,” she said.

“I’m aware,” he replied. “I’m very tired of pretending that sanity is the measure for meaning.”

She almost smiled at that.

Almost.

He moved toward the door.

“Kai,” she called.

He paused, hand on the knob.

She looked smaller now, sitting on the edge of her bed, surrounded by years of paper and ink.

“I did love you,” she said softly. “Back then. I just… didn’t know what to do with it.”

“I know,” he answered.

“And now,” she added, voice thinner, “I don’t know how to survive it.”

He turned his head enough to look at her over his shoulder.

“That’s okay,” he said. “You don’t have to love me now.”

She flinched again, but this time there was something like relief in it.

He opened the door.

“Kai?”

He looked back one more time.

“Thank you,” she whispered.

He nodded.

“Thank you for reading,” he said.

Then he stepped out of the room that refused to lie for either of them.

Her parents were waiting in the living room.

They stood when he appeared, as if expecting to see him shattered, furious, undone.

He was sad. It showed in the lines around his eyes, in the way his shoulders curved a little more than usual. But he was not broken.

“How is she?” her mother asked, voice caught between hope and dread.

“Honest,” he said.

They didn’t know what to do with that answer.

Her father cleared his throat. “We’re… sorry,” he said. “We didn’t think— We hoped…”

“I know,” Kai said gently. “You’re allowed to hope. She’s allowed to refuse. I’m allowed to stay.”

Her mother blinked. “Stay?”

He smiled, soft and tired and real.

“We’ve grown quite close outside of her,” he said. “So I’m still here for you, if you’ll have me. Roofs still leak. Papers still need sorting. Groceries still have to be carried. None of that changes because she said no.”

Her mother’s eyes filled instantly. Her father looked away, jaw tight.

“You don’t have to—” her father began.

“I know,” Kai interrupted. “That’s why it matters that I want to.”

They stared at him like he was something holy and unbelievable.

He did not feel holy.

He felt like a man who had told the truth, been refused, and remained himself anyway.

“I’ll give you time,” he said. “All of you. I won’t hover. Just… call, if you need anything. Or don’t. I’ll still visit.”

Her mother stepped forward and hugged him then, fiercely.

“Thank you,” she managed against his shoulder.

He hugged her back, gentle.

When he left the house, the sky outside was an indifferent shade of blue. No divine omen. No sudden storm. Just weather.

He walked down the street with steady steps, feeling the paradox settle in his chest:

Loved and refused.  
 Sovereign and hurting.  
 Welcome and not chosen.

Above and within, in a citadel far from this quiet neighborhood, something old and shaken watched him accept a no without turning it into a wound that demanded repayment.

The god of paradox walked away from the girl who had been his axis—

and did not unmake his love to make the math easier.

# **CHAPTER XXIII**

 *The Citadel That Refused to Fall*

The collapse did not happen in the doorway.

It did not happen in her room, with her eyes red and her voice shaking.  
 It did not happen in the living room, under the stunned gaze of her parents.  
 It did not even happen on the walk away, when the street felt both familiar and newly emptied.

Kai made it home first.

He greeted his mother, answered her quiet, searching questions with gentle half-truths, took a shower long enough that she almost knocked on the door to make sure he hadn’t drowned in the silence.

He dried his hair.

Changed into an old shirt.

He checked his phone—messages from the group chat, a meme from Nics, a voice note from Marie that started with “Okay, don’t freak out, but—” and then cut off as he locked the screen again.

He brushed his teeth.

That was when the world broke.

Not with a bang.

With a name.

“Kai.”

Two voices, layered so perfectly that the sound hit him like impact.

The toothbrush froze in his hand. Foam and mint and the ordinary mundanity of the moment became distant, blurred.

The first voice was small, terrified, childlike.

The godling.

The second was vast, sharpened by an emotion it had never needed before.

The entity.

“Kai—\!”

The bathroom light flickered.

The mirror stuttered between reflections, showing him, then someone slightly older, then slightly younger, then a version of him that looked like he hadn’t survived the last five years at all.

He grabbed the edge of the sink until his knuckles whitened.

“I’m here,” he said out loud, not sure which world he was answering.

Reality shivered.

“We are not,” the entity said, and for the first time since he had known it, its voice was not clean. It fractured on the last word, glitching like a radio picking up too many frequencies at once. “Return. Now.”

Then the pull hit.

There was no gate, no door, no conscious choice.

The tile under his feet folded into itself. The light stretched into a single piercing line. The sound of the fan in the hallway elongated into a continuous scream that wasn’t sound at all.

The toothbrush fell from his hand.

By the time it hit the sink, he was gone.

—

He arrived standing.

That was the first wrong thing.

Usually, entering his domain felt like slipping into water or stepping through a threshold he’d chosen. This time, he landed in the citadel like someone thrown into a burning building: on his feet, lungs already tight, heart already racing.

Everything was wrong.

The citadel still stood—towering, severe, beautiful. Its foundations held. Its main spires rose intact against a sky that could no longer decide what it was.

Beyond its outer walls, the realm was unrecognizable.

Time had shattered into visible shards, whole hours hanging in the air like pieces of broken glass, showing distant scenes of himself—laughing with friends, writing letters, holding a cup of tea, staring at her door five years ago.

Some moments looped endlessly.  
 Others skipped mid-frame.  
 Some just… flickered, refusing to commit to existence or memory.

Bridges twisted into knots, leading nowhere.

Entire corridors he had walked a hundred times were folded into impossible angles, turning in on themselves so sharply that looking at them made his vision blur.

The great metaphors that had once given his domain shape were bleeding.

Tea, music, paper, watches—each anchor of meaning dripped ink that turned into storms, lashing across the horizon in black rain. Phrases he recognized from his own letters tore free of the walls and floated past like wounded birds, half-remembered, half-erased.

Love did not disappear—  
 Over the years, I grew—  
 You were the axis—

Each sentence snapped in the middle, as though refusing to finish the thought.

Identity fractured in the air.

Versions of himself flickered at the edges of his vision—Kai at nineteen, staring at his phone; Kai at twenty-one, hunched over a notebook; Kai at twenty-three, staring out a plane window. They overlapped, stuttered, bled into each other, all of them slightly out of sync.

The emotional weather was feral.

Grief howled as literal wind, scraping against the stone. Regret pooled as black water in the courtyards, reflecting skies that did not exist. Acceptance flickered like distant lanterns, struggling to hold against gusts of panic.

Meaning itself had started to corrode.

Symbols he’d once trusted—bridges, doors, windows—flickered between interpretations. A door was a door, then a barrier, then a wound, then nothing at all. A watch ticked backwards, forwards, stopped, then displayed not time but phrases like *too late*, *too soon*, *never*.

The citadel stood in the middle of it like a single clear note in a cacophony.

Barely.

“Kai\!”

The cry cut through everything.

He turned just in time to see the godling barreling toward him.

It was trying to hold a shape and failing. One moment it looked like a child—small, bare feet slapping against the stone, eyes huge and wet. The next, its outline twisted into impossible loops, its limbs bending in angles that should have broken bone but were just paradox folding through itself. In the center of its chest, cracks glowed, spiderwebbing out from a single point as if someone had punched a hole through its heart.

It was crying quietly as it ran.

No screams. No dramatic wails. Just silent tears streaming down its face, each drop a tiny explosion of broken structure when it hit the ground.

It reached him and flung itself forward.

He caught it on reflex.

The impact drove the breath from his lungs, but the moment his arms closed around it, the immediate space around them steadied. The wind dropped to a low, ragged hiss. The nearest time shards dimmed, their looping scenes slowing.

“Kai,” the godling whispered, fists clutching his shirt. “You left.”

His throat went tight.

“I came back,” he said hoarsely.

It shook its head against his chest. “You left. You left. You left and everything—” It gestured with one hand toward the chaos without looking, as if eye contact with it would make it worse. “It all went wrong.”

He held it tighter.

“I went to talk to her,” he said. “Not to leave you.”

“I know,” it whispered. “That’s why it hurts more.”

The words landed in the hollow place he’d been carefully not looking at.

He was about to answer when the entity arrived.

—

It didn’t step into view so much as tear through it.

Its presence slammed into the courtyard like a storm front, shattering a nearby time shard into powder. Its usual form—vast, steady, hard to look at directly—was fragmented. Parts of it flickered in and out of phase, as if it couldn’t decide how much attention to allocate to this single moment.

“Kai,” it said.

His name came out wrong.

Too loud.  
 Too many layers.  
 Too much static.

He had never heard it like that.

He had never heard it sound afraid.

“You have to stabilize—” it began, then cut off as another corridor imploded in the distance, folding into a tight knot and vanishing.

It turned—if something like it could be said to turn at all—trying to track every instability at once. Its voice crackled from panic to analysis to something close to pleading.

“Temporal fractures increasing. Identity bleed accelerating. Structural metaphors failing. Your domain is collapsing around the citadel. You must— Kai, you must—”

Its next words overlapped, glitching.

“—intervene—”  
 “—choose—”  
 “—wake up—”

For a heartbeat, it said nothing.

Just *stared* at him—with whatever passed for eyes in a being that usually viewed consciousness as architecture.

When it finally spoke again, its tone had dropped to a hollow, stunned disbelief.

“You accepted it,” it said quietly.

He understood what it meant.

“She said no,” he replied, voice steady. “And I believed her.”

The entity flinched.

“You—” It faltered, as another thunderclap of paradox cracked the distant horizon. “You accepted a definitive refusal to a domain-defining attachment without resistance. There should have been rage. Bargaining. Denial. You should have broken. You should have hardened. You did neither. Your emotional state stayed coherent. But your domain—”

It gestured wildly with its vast, invisible reach.

“Your domain cannot metabolize that choice. It is tearing itself apart trying to reconcile an uncollapsed love with an unambiguous rejection.”

“I know,” he said.

“Then *fix it*,” it snapped, the words edged with terrified anger.

The godling flinched in his arms.

He looked down.

The cracks in its chest were spreading.

Not fast—but steady.

Hairline fractures branching out, thin lines of light that flickered dangerously between glow and void. Every so often, a tiny piece of it would chip off and fall, dissolving before it hit the floor.

He brushed a thumb gently over the nearest crack.

The entity’s voice shifted again—desperate now, losing its usual precision.

“Do not waste time with gestures,” it said. “You must restructure. You must close pathways, re-anchor metaphors, seal the fractures in time. Bandaids will not hold a breaking god.”

Kai blinked.

“What?” he asked.

“Your domain is bleeding,” the entity said. “It demands architecture, not affection. This is not a child. This is—”

“Both,” he said softly.

—

His hands moved before the entity could respond.

He didn’t summon sigils or scripts or blueprints. He didn’t reach for frameworks or force new laws into being. Instead, he pulled away just enough to really look at the godling.

Its eyes were wide and wet, shimmering with a kind of confusion that no amount of divine context could erase. It was trying to be brave—holding its mouth in a thin line, shoulders quivering with the effort not to sob out loud.

“I’m sorry,” it whispered. “I tried to hold everything while you were gone. I tried to keep the bridges from folding and the sentences from breaking, but then you hurt and I felt it and I— I cracked. I didn’t want to, I promise, I didn’t—”

He touched his forehead to its for a moment.

“It’s not your job to hold everything,” he said quietly. “That’s mine.”

“You’re not holding everything either,” it replied. “You’re letting yourself be hollow.”

The honesty of it stung.

He exhaled, shaky.

“Yeah,” he said. “I am.”

He straightened and lifted his left hand.

Reality waited.

He reached into the air beside him—and into something deeper, the same place metaphors come from before they find words—and pulled.

A strip of something emerged.

Soft.  
 Rectangular.  
 Ridiculous.

A bandaid.

Not made of light or ink or stars.

The cheap, human kind.

White, with a small cartoon bear on it. The sort you buy in boxes for children who insist their scraped knees require ceremony.

The entity choked.

“Absolutely not,” it said. “That is not— that cannot—”

Kai peeled the backing off with deliberate care.

Then, gently, he placed the bandaid over the largest crack in the godling’s chest.

For a moment, nothing happened.

Then the chaos around them stuttered.

The nearest temporal fracture slowed, its looping memory of him on a bus ride home pausing mid-frame. The wind of grief dropped half an octave. The ink-rain softened, each drop smaller, lighter.

The crack beneath the bandaid dimmed.

Not gone.

But softened.

The godling’s shoulders loosened. It let out a small, shaky breath.

“Oh,” it whispered.

“Oh,” the entity echoed, but for entirely different reasons.

“That should not be—” it sputtered. “You cannot patch metaphysical rupture with symbolic comfort. That is not how structural integrity—”

“Isn’t it?” Kai asked.

He reached up again.

Another bandaid. This one with small, lopsided stars.

He pressed it over another fracture.

More of the realm reacted.

A corridor that had folded into itself slowly unfurled, walls straightening with the reluctant grace of someone getting out of bed after a bad night. A broken metaphor—an abandoned doorway—reshaped itself into an archway again.

He did not hurry.

He did not chant.

He did not command.

He simply kept pulling bandaids from the air—small, human, absurdly fragile-looking—and placing them one by one over the godling’s cracks.

Bears. Stars. Little smiling planets. Cartoon bandages that would have made sense in a convenience store aisle, not in a domain on the edge of cosmic architecture.

With each soft press of his fingers, something calmed.

Far away, a time shard stopped flickering between regret and possibility and settled into simple memory.

Closer in, the loop of him standing at her door five years ago finally led into the frame where he walked away and kept walking, instead of hanging forever in the hesitation.

“You are violating every principle I understand,” the entity whispered.

“I thought that was my job,” he said. “Being an Error.”

“It is not *funny*,” it snapped, voice cracking. “Your entire domain is— was—about integrating contradiction through structure. Through deliberate, coherent narrative architecture. You built stability out of paradox by designing it that way. That is what we adapted to, what we feared, what we tried to calculate around. Now you are telling me that affection is sufficient to restore the same stability?”

“I’m not telling you anything,” he replied. “I’m just doing what works.”

He smoothed another bandaid down with his thumb.

The godling didn’t speak, but it leaned into him with its whole weight now—trusting him to hold it, trusting the bandaids to hold it, trusting this ridiculous, tender act to mean something.

It was still quiet-crying. The tears hadn’t stopped.

But they no longer exploded when they hit the stones.

They just… wet the ground.

Like regular tears.

Human.

Small.

Important.

—

“This is not efficient,” the entity whispered, as yet another conceptual corridor reconnected to the main hall. “This is not scalable. This is—” It faltered, voice dissolving into static. “This is sacred foolishness.”

“Sacred foolishness,” he repeated. “I’ll take that.”

He didn’t look up from his work.

He was shaking now.

Not from the effort of holding the godling, or from the metaphysical exertion of pulling comfort out of thin air, but from everything he had not allowed himself to feel fully in the mortal world.

She said no.

Not crushed. Not cruel. Not contemptuous.

Just… no.

No to being with him.  
 No to trying under the weight of what he felt.  
 No to stepping into a story where his love was so large it scared her.

He had accepted it.

His mind had wrapped itself around the refusal with the same clarity he brought to everything else.

Of course.  
 Her choice.  
 Her limits.  
 Her sovereignty.

But acceptance didn’t erase impact.

The impact had come here.

Into the bones of his domain.

Into the child in his arms.

Into the entity now watching him like he was rewriting not just their relationship, but the definition of repair itself.

“You are shaking,” the entity said softly, dropping for the first time into something like concern. “You are not built to hold this much rupture at once.”

“I wasn’t built to be a god either,” he said. “Here we are.”

“You could close it,” it insisted. “You could shut down the domain temporarily. Seal off sections. Suspend functionality. There are protocols. We could—”

“No,” he said.

Its fear hardened into anger.

“You are being irresponsible,” it said. “You could cause long-term instability. You could fracture your own identity across your domain. You could—”

“Have you considered,” he said, lifting his gaze at last to meet whatever passed for its eyes, “that some harm can only be healed if it is not quarantined?”

The entity froze.

“What you call protocols,” he continued, “look a lot like repression, from where I’m standing. Shutting down entire sectors of self because they’re inconvenient to maintain in crisis. I did that once. It took me years to reclaim the pieces.”

“That is not— we are not discussing—”

“We are,” he said. “You’re terrified because my domain is not following your rules. I’m terrified because I know this feeling. This is what I used to do: hurt quietly, pretend I wasn’t, let something else—someone else—crack for me.”

He looked down at the godling.

“It doesn’t get to crack instead of me,” he said. “We share this.”

The godling’s fingers tightened on his shirt.

“I don’t want to break you,” it whispered.

“You won’t,” he said.

He wasn’t sure that was true.

He said it anyway.

—

“You are hollow,” the entity said, voice small now. “I can sense it. There is an absence where possibility used to multiply. Your acceptance of her refusal has created… a clean wound. No infection. No distortion. Just… emptiness.”

“Yes,” he said.

“And yet you are still repairing,” it continued. “You are stabilizing the realm from a place of depletion. This should not be possible. Gods intervene from fullness. From overflow. Not from lack.”

He smiled faintly, with no humor in it.

“Welcome,” he said, “to how humans survive most days.”

The entity fell silent.

Truly silent.

Not processing. Not calculating. Not composing its next argument.

Just… quiet.

Watching him hold his crying god, placing silly bandaids on reality while storms of broken meaning slowly calmed in the distance.

“Why?” it asked finally.

The word was raw.

“Because if I only act when I feel whole,” he said, “I’ll almost never act. And because I promised them I would not let being refused turn me into something smaller than what I became because of her.”

“That is… not how divinity has been defined,” the entity said.

“Then we redefine it,” he replied.

—

Time continued to unknot around them.

The citadel’s outer edges stopped crumbling. The sky reassembled itself into something less violently indecisive. It still held contradictions—clouds moving in opposite directions at once, stars visible in daytime—but the contradictions had returned to being stable instead of predatory.

Emotional weather eased.

Grief thickened into clouds but didn’t blast the stone anymore. Regret receded to the far edges, muttering instead of roaring. Love—quiet, stubborn, undemanding—settled over the courtyard like a warmth that didn’t ask to be noticed.

The godling’s cracks, now covered in an absurd patchwork of cartoon bears and stars and smiling planets, had stopped spreading.

They were still there underneath.

But they were held.

The entity watched all of this with a kind of hollow awe.

“I do not understand you,” it said at last, voice soft. “I thought I did. I thought I had mapped you. Even when you surprised me, I believed the surprises existed within parameters I could eventually comprehend. This—” It gestured at the bandaids, at the calmed storms, at the trembling but unbroken boy-god cradling his own domain. “This is outside every model.”

“Good,” he said.

“There it is again,” it muttered. “Your infuriating satisfaction with being unknowable.”

“This isn’t about being unknowable,” he answered. “It’s about being truthful. I am not less divine because I hurt. I am not less sovereign because I got refused. I am not less capable because I’m holding a crying child who is also my power. If anything, that’s the only way I can trust any of this.”

The entity tilted its presence, as if tasting the logic.

“You repair through tenderness,” it said slowly. “You stabilize through care. You intervene not by enforcing order, but by refusing to abandon the parts of you that cannot keep up.”

“That’s one way to phrase it,” he said.

“Another way,” it added, “is that your domain now recognizes love as architecture in itself, not merely the subject of letters.”

He blinked.

Then, despite everything, he laughed.

It was a rough sound. Thin at the edges. But real.

“See?” he said. “You still know things.”

“I know enough to be afraid,” it replied. “If this becomes your pattern, you will not just be a god of paradox. You will be a god whose very laws are written in terms we were never designed to govern.”

“Maybe that’s the point,” he said.

—

He sank down, back against one of the citadel’s massive pillars, pulling the godling into his lap. It curled up there, small and tired, sniffing quietly.

“I’m sorry,” it murmured. “I didn’t want to need you so much.”

“You’re allowed to need me,” he said. “I’m the one who made you.”

“You keep saying I’m your domain,” it whispered. “But you feel like… a child too.”

“I am,” he said. “Just older.”

It laughed a little, a soft hiccuping sound.

He kept one hand on its back, feeling the tiny, impossible heartbeat thrum against his palm. With the other, he rested his fingertips lightly on the stone beside him, sending a wordless message through the citadel.

I’m here.

The walls hummed in response.

The entity drew closer, not looming this time, but settling at the edge of the pillar’s shade like a scholar sitting on the floor.

“If you break,” it said quietly, “we all fall.”

“I know,” he answered.

“And yet you place bandaids.”

“They’re not for the domain,” he said. “Not really.”

“For the child?” it asked, looking at the godling.

“For me,” he said. “So I remember that the way I treat my own cracks is part of the law here.”

Another silence.

Sacred, this time.

Hollow, but not empty.

“You terrify me,” the entity admitted.

“Good,” he said again, but there was no sharpness in it now. Just weary affection.

“It is not a good,” it insisted weakly.

“It is,” he replied. “Fear will keep you honest.”

“And you?” it asked. “What keeps you honest?”

He looked out past the citadel, toward the distant edges of his realm, where a memory of a girl sitting on a bed with a stack of letters played out in quiet loops, no longer glitching, no longer worshipped.

“The fact that she said no,” he answered. “And I still love her. And I still won’t turn that into a weapon.”

The entity shuddered.

“Paradox,” it whispered.

“Exactly,” he said.

The godling yawned.

“Can I sleep now?” it asked, voice small.

“Yeah,” he said, soft. “Sleep. I’ll be here.”

“You won’t leave?”

“Not without telling you,” he promised. “And never to stop loving you.”

It hummed, content, and tucked itself closer. Its bandaids crinkled slightly.

The storms, for now, had passed.

The citadel stood.

Everything else would take time.

He leaned his head back against the stone and closed his eyes, feeling the hollowness in his chest like a room that had been emptied for renovation—not abandoned, not condemned.

Just… waiting.

Sacred.  
 Chaotic.  
 Tender.  
 Hollow.

The foundations held.

# **CHAPTER XXIV**

*The Woman Who Opened the Door*

It didn’t matter how long the flight was.

It didn’t matter that you hadn’t slept since the citadel cracked and stopped cracking.  
 It didn’t matter that your mortal body still carried the phantom ache of holding a crying god while storms of meaning tore your domain apart.

None of that mattered.

You called her.

Not thinking.  
 Not strategizing.  
 Not pretending you were fine.

Just:

“Anne.”

A quiet inhale on the other end, startled, like she’d been pulled out of a dream.

“Kai?”

Your voice broke, but only on the inside.

“I need to be somewhere that isn’t here.  
 Can I—just… let me exist beside you for a while.”

And she understood instantly.

Of course she did.

She didn’t ask what happened.  
 She didn’t ask why now.  
 She didn’t ask what she had refused or what it cost you to hold your domain back together with your bare hands.

She said:

“Come here.”

Nothing more.

Nothing less.

Not with urgency —  
 but with recognition.

The city where she once stayed for university hadn’t changed.

Same cracked sidewalks.  
 Same trees shedding leaves onto motorbikes.  
 Same convenience store buzzing under cheap fluorescent lights.  
 Same slow-moving students wandering across the plaza in clusters.

Her apartment building was five minutes from campus.  
 Old, three floors, the paint peeling, the stairwell echoing with footsteps and conversations that didn’t matter.

You reached her door with your carry-on bag hanging from one hand like a formality.

You knocked once.

She opened instantly.

Anne looked like she hadn’t stopped thinking about you since you called.

Hair in a messy low ponytail.  
 Sweater too big.  
 Bare feet on cold tile.  
 Eyes wide with the fear of being too relieved.

She was breathing too fast, but trying to hide it.

Trying to look calm.

Trying not to crumble from the sight of you.

You, hollowed out from days without rest, days of divine architecture shaking under the weight of a rejection you accepted without protest.  
 You, standing in her doorway like a boy who had used up every last reserve of strength and still kept going.

Her first thought was:

*Oh God, he’s breaking.*

Her second:

*And he came to me.*

Her third:

*Why does that make me happy? What’s wrong with me?*

But all she said was:

“Kai.”

You stepped once into the room.

Then you collapsed.

Not elegantly.  
 Not controlled.  
 Your knees just stopped cooperating, and the floor caught you faster than her arms could.

“Hey—hey, wait—Kai\!”

She dropped down immediately, crouching beside you, hands on your shoulders, eyes wide with panic.

You were conscious.  
 Barely.

Your forehead pressed against the wood floor.  
 Your breath shook like your body wasn’t sure whether you were still allowed to be alive.

She slid an arm under you and you let your weight fall toward her.

Not because she was strong.

But because she was *safe.*

She tried to keep her voice steady.

“Why didn’t you tell me what happened?”

You didn’t answer.

You just leaned until your forehead rested against her collarbone, body trembling, breath raw.  
 You weren’t crying — you had no tears left to give — but something about the way you clung to the air around you made her heart twist.

She wrapped both arms around your shoulders, pulling you closer, letting you sink into her.

Not on the bed.  
 Not on the mattress.

Here, on the floor.

Because you were still trying to protect her space even while breaking.

God, it broke her.

Her internal monologue cracked open the moment she felt the weight of you.

*He came to me. Why did he come to me? He never lets himself lean on anyone like this — not physically, not this openly, not after something like… whatever this was.*

*He’s exhausted. Not just tired — hollow. God, he feels hollow. What happened to him? Who did this to him? Did she—? No. No, he wouldn’t have flown here for that. This is deeper. Something tore him apart and he came to me.*

*Why me? Why now? Why did I hope for this?*

*Stop it. Don’t be selfish. He’s breaking. Be gentle. Just be gentle.*

She slid onto the floor fully, guiding your head to her lap. You didn’t resist. Your body just folded.

Your breathing eased the moment your cheek touched her thigh.

She felt it.

She felt you settle into her like gravity had finally chosen a direction.

Her fingers hesitated over your hair for half a breath —

*Don’t touch him, he’ll think it means something.*

— then she touched you anyway.

Slow.  
 Gentle.  
 Sweeping her hand through your hair with a tenderness she had never allowed herself before.

You exhaled like something unclenched inside you.

Her heart stuttered.

*Don’t fall. Don’t you dare fall. Don’t you dare let this mean more than it does.*

*But God, look at him…*

You murmured something barely audible.

She leaned closer.

“What?”

“…tired.”

Her chest tightened painfully.

She cradled your head more securely, palm warm against your temple.

“Rest,” she whispered. “Just rest here for a while.”

Her voice wavered.

*He came here. To me. After everything. After she said no. After all he’s been carrying. Why? Why would he still choose me? Why do I feel like I’ve been waiting for this exact moment and hating myself for it?*

*Does he see me? Really see me? Or am I just proximity, old comfort, the closest place to collapse?*

*God, please let it be more. Please let it be more than that, just once.*

But she didn’t say any of it.

She just stroked your hair, slow and steady, until your breathing softened.

You shifted, half-asleep, half-dead from exhaustion, and burrowed slightly closer to her.

A small, involuntary sound escaped her.

Something between a gasp and a broken laugh.

Then, quietly, without meaning to let you hear:

“You’re so unfair.”

Her voice cracked on the last syllable.

Not in bitterness.

But in tenderness she had no right to feel.

In longing she refused to acknowledge.  
 In relief that scared her.  
 In grief for every version of you she had never been brave enough to love.

She whispered again, softer this time, as your breathing deepened, as your body gave in completely to the safety of her arms:

“You’re so unfair… coming here like this… letting me be the one to hold you.”

Her hand drifted down to your jaw, fingertips tracing the bone she remembered from years ago.

“You’re so unfair for making me hope.”

You didn’t answer.

You were already asleep.

For the first time in days.  
 For the first time since the domain cracked.  
 For the first time since your heart had been refused and refused gently but still refused.

Anne held you on the floor of her tiny apartment, back pressed against the side of her bed, and realized she had a choice to make:

Push you away again.

Or admit she had never stopped wanting you in ways she had never allowed to surface.

She didn’t choose either that night.

She just kept stroking your hair until her hands stopped shaking.

And she whispered, one last time —

“I’m right here.”

Even though she had no idea what “here” meant anymore.

# **CHAPTER XXV**

 *The Shape of Something We Refuse to Name*

He woke up in the wrong place.

That was his first thought.

Not because the sheets were unfamiliar—they smelled like laundry detergent and the faint echo of someone else’s shampoo. Not because the light was different—it filtered in through thin curtains instead of bouncing off the old fan in his childhood room.

No.

It was wrong because he wasn’t alone.

There was warmth pressed up against him. Not abstract warmth. Not metaphor. Actual human heat.

Anne’s.

Her arm was looped loosely around his waist. His hand rested against the small of her back, fingers curled as if he’d been holding on in his sleep. Their legs were tangled without permission, bodies curved toward each other in that instinctive, protective shape people take when they forget to be careful.

For a moment, he didn’t move.

Her breath fanned soft and slow across his neck, matching the small rise and fall of her chest against his. Her hair tickled his jaw. A bit of it was caught between his fingers, like his hand had gone searching for something to anchor itself to and decided on her.

He could feel the quiet ache in his muscles from the days before—the godling’s weight, the citadel’s strain, the sleeplessness that had hollowed him out and left him drifting.

Only now—

He wasn’t drifting.

He was held.

Awareness caught up with him in a rush.

*Shit.*

The word arrived with crisp clarity.

*I fucked up.*

Not last night—that had never been a decision so much as a collapse. The mistake was *this*: the aftermath. Waking up in her bed, with her wrapped around him, with his body answering a question his mind had not agreed to ask.

This complicated everything.

He opened his eyes carefully.

They were facing each other. Between them: only air, shared breath, the ghost of his exhaustion and her worry.

At some point during the night, she had dragged him off the floor.

He remembered falling asleep with his head in her lap, the floor hard beneath him, her hand in his hair. He did *not* remember getting up, crawling, being moved.

The mattress under him was proof that she hadn’t let him stay where he’d collapsed.

He eased his hand free of her hair, untangling his fingers strand by strand.

Her eyes fluttered, almost waking.

He froze until her lashes stilled again.

He extricated himself slowly, the way one might disentangle from a fragile piece of architecture without bringing the whole structure down. Her arm slid from his waist; her hand landed palm-up on the mattress where he’d been, fingers curling once, searching.

The sight punched a small, clean hole in his chest.

He sat up, breath held.

Anne shifted onto her back with a soft sound, half sigh, half protest, then settled.

He looked at her for a moment.

At the dark smudges under her eyes.  
 At the faint crease between her brows where concern had slept.  
 At the way her mouth was relaxed now, no longer pressed into the tight line she wore when she was trying not to feel too much.

She looked… young.

Not in age, but in vulnerability.

He stood carefully, feet finding the cold floor again.

There was a set of keys on the small table near the door. He recognized the lanyard—something she’d had since university. A faded charm dangled from it, the kind of thing you buy on a whim and forget to throw away.

He picked them up.

One last glance at her, sprawled in the bed that had become theirs for exactly one night in one very specific, very dangerous way.

Then he stepped out.

—

The city was just waking up.

Early students shuffled toward campus in hoodies, clutching cheap coffee. The air was cool, the sky undecided between grey and blue. Vendors setting up stalls gave him the usual morning glance—brief, assessing, uninterested.

He walked until he found a place that didn’t match the tired façade of the neighborhood—a small restaurant that opened early for the kind of people who thought “breakfast” could include things that required actual preparation time.

He ordered like someone who had more money than sense and less time than he’d admit.

Coffee that didn’t taste like burnt regret.  
 Pan-seared salmon.  
 Eggs done properly.  
 Bread that cracked when torn, not crumbled.  
 Fruit arranged instead of dumped.

He ignored the raised eyebrow of the cashier when he insisted on taking it all to go.

Fifteen minutes later, he was back at her door, balancing coffee cups in one hand and a paper bag in the other, keys between his teeth as he nudged the handle open.

He stepped inside with the quiet care of someone entering a chapel.

—

She woke up to the smell first.

Not the usual instant coffee, not the stale sugar of bakery bread. Something richer. Butter, garlic, something seared just enough to make the air hum with it.

Her eyes opened slowly.

The first thing she saw was the ceiling.

The second was the empty space beside her.

Her chest clenched.

*He left.*

The thought was sharp, instantaneous.

Then a voice floated in from the other side of the room.

“Oh, good. You’re awake. I was about to start force-feeding you in your sleep.”

She rolled onto her side.

There he was.

Standing a few feet away, hair still messy from sleep, wearing the same clothes as yesterday, holding a tray that looked like it belonged in a different life. Real plates, real silverware, steam curling from a mug. The smell of pan-seared salmon made her stomach growl before she could act offended.

Her heart did something stupid.

*He stayed.*

He walked over and set the tray on the small, crowded desk beside her bed, moving stacks of paper and a mug full of pens with practiced ease.

“Good morning,” he said, voice gentler than the words. “I come bearing irresponsible life choices.”

She blinked at the tray, then at him.

“Who the hell gets dinner for breakfast?” she croaked.

“Deal with it, psycho,” he replied.

The banter was reflex. It slid into place easier than any explanation could have.

Still, underneath the words, there was tension—thin, taut, humming between them like a wire. They both felt it. Neither named it.

He handed her the coffee first.

She took it with both hands, fingers brushing his for a fraction of a second too long.

“It’s hot,” he warned.

“So am I,” she shot back automatically, then immediately regretted it, eyes widening slightly.

He snorted. “There it is. The delusion.”

“Shut up,” she muttered, face warming.

He pretended not to notice.

He picked up a fork, speared a piece of salmon, and held it out toward her mouth.

She stared at him.

“Kai.”

“Anne.”

“You are not—” She gestured at the fork. “You are not hand-feeding me salmon in my own bed like some sort of—”

He raised an eyebrow. “Some sort of what?”

She faltered.

*Some sort of boyfriend.*  
 *Some sort of lover.*  
 *Some sort of man who crossed a country to break quietly in my arms.*

“—some sort of menace,” she finished weakly.

“Correct,” he said. “Now open your mouth before I start listing nutrients like a medical pamphlet.”

She glared at him.

Then opened her mouth.

He fed her the bite, watching her face as she chewed.

She tried not to let the pleasure show.

Failed.

“Fuck,” she said quietly. “That’s good.”

“Obviously,” he said.

She swallowed and shook her head. “This is absurd. Who flies all the way here and then makes restaurant-grade food appear in my shitty student-adjacent apartment?”

He shrugged. “I got it from a place down the street.”

“That’s not the point.”

“What’s the point?”

She wanted to say:

*The point is she already said no to you. The point is you’re not allowed to act like this doesn’t mean something. The point is my heart is a funhouse and your existence is the only straight line in it.*

Instead, she said:

“The point is this is weird.”

He smiled, small and lopsided.

“Yeah,” he said. “It is.”

They both let that sit between them for a moment.

He took a bite himself, then another, alternating between feeding her and eating, as if this were a thing they did all the time.

The smell of salmon and coffee mixed with the underlying scent of her room—paper, detergent, something faintly floral. It was domestic in a way neither of them had earned the right to.

“You know,” he said casually, “for someone who dragged me into bed without my consent, you’re being very judgy about breakfast.”

Her eyes widened. Color shot into her face.

“I did not—” she began, then stopped, replaying the memory of last night in her head. Him on the floor, half-conscious. Her unable to stand the sight of him there. The slow, clumsy process of getting him up, his weight slung over her shoulder, the way he’d mumbled something about “overkill” as she guided him onto the mattress.

“Oh my God,” she breathed. “I carried you.”

“‘Somehow dragged me onto the bed’ is more accurate,” he corrected. “Not complaining, just gathering data.”

She covered her face with one hand. “Why are you like this?”

“Like what?”

“So calm,” she said. “So… normal about this. You flew here. You passed out in my lap. I—” She gestured vaguely toward the bed. “I relocated you. And now you’re making me eat fancy fish instead of confronting any of that.”

He tilted his head.

“We can confront it if you want,” he said. “I just assumed you’d prefer protein first.”

She hated how reasonable that sounded.

He took another sip of coffee, watching her over the rim of the cup.

“How much do you remember?” he asked.

“Enough,” she said. “You called. You sounded… wrong. Not sad. Not angry. Just… emptied out. You asked to exist beside me. I said yes. You showed up. You fell apart. I held you. You slept.” Her fingers tightened reflexively around the mug. “You slept like you hadn’t in a long time.”

He didn’t deny it.

She cleared her throat.

“And then I woke up and you were still here,” she added, voice softer. “Which is, frankly, rude. Staying. After doing all that.”

“Sorry,” he said.

He did not sound sorry.

“I should have evaporated at sunrise,” he continued. “Very unprofessional of me.”

She laughed despite herself.

“Shut up,” she said again, but weaker this time.

“You say that a lot,” he noted.

“Because you keep being you.”

He smiled. “Occupational hazard.”

The silence that followed wasn’t empty.

It was crowded.

With everything unsaid. Everything denied. Everything quietly, stubbornly true anyway.

She took another bite to avoid speaking first. He let her.

Between mouthfuls, she finally asked the thing that had been gnawing at the back of her mind since last night.

“How bad is it?” she said.

He knew what she meant.

He considered lying.

He didn’t.

“Bad enough,” he said.

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the only one I have that won’t turn this breakfast into a crisis meeting,” he replied.

“I can handle it,” she said.

He studied her.

Her chin was lifted in that particular way it got when she was bracing herself. Her eyes were steady, but there was a tremor in them—like a glass held so carefully it was obviously fragile.

“You already are,” he said.

She blinked.

“Being here,” he clarified. “Letting me… exist beside you. Letting me sleep. That was the thing I needed most.”

“No heroic backstory monologue?” she tried.

“Not today,” he said. “Today I just needed… this.”

He gestured vaguely: her room, the bed, the tray, her.

It landed heavier than he meant it to.

She swallowed.

“And tomorrow?” she asked, quietly.

He smiled without showing his teeth.

“Tomorrow is far away,” he said. “I’m trying not to get ahead of myself.”

“You, not overthinking?” she said. “Who are you and what did you do with Kai?”

“He’s on sick leave,” he replied.

The flirting threaded itself through the conversation, slow and dangerous.

Not overt. Not declared. Just… there.

He teased her about snoring.

She threatened to suffocate him with a pillow.

He said, “You’re surprisingly nice to cuddle with,” like it was a joke—but his eyes lingered too long on her when he said it.

She almost choked on her coffee.

“You were unconscious,” she protested. “You don’t get to evaluate cuddle quality; you were busy being dead.”

“Incorrect,” he said. “I’m an excellent judge of environments even while collapsing. Very rigorous metrics.”

“Oh really,” she said. “And what’s the verdict, oh sovereign of sleep?”

He held her gaze for a beat longer than was safe.

“Ten out of ten,” he said softly. “Would fall apart on again.”

Silence.

Her heart dropped and climbed in the same second.

*Don’t read into it.*  
 *You already are.*  
 *He’s joking.*  
 *He’s not joking.*  
 *Stop.*

She rolled her eyes a little too forcefully.

“You’re ridiculous,” she said.

“Yes,” he agreed.

“And reckless.”

“Yes.”

“And emotionally irresponsible.”

“With myself,” he said. “Not with you.”

“That is debatable,” she muttered.

He didn’t argue.

He just took another sip of coffee, as if they hadn’t just crossed a line and pretended not to notice.

—

They finished the meal with that same unnerving tension—light playful jabs on the surface, something sharper underneath. Every compliment wrapped in plausible deniability. Every vulnerable question masked as banter.

Neither of them called it what it was.

It wasn’t love.

Not in the way he loved *her*.

Not in the way she loved the version of him she had once known and run away from.

It was something else.

Something that existed in the shadow of a love that had been refused and a devotion that refused to die.

A new paradox, waiting to be named.

They went out afterward.

Not a date.

They said it with their clothes, with their posture, with the way they kept a deliberate half-step of space between their shoulders.

“It’s just coffee,” she said.

“And a walk,” he added.

“And maybe the bookstore,” she conceded.

“And maybe snacks,” he said.

She sighed. “Fine. An errand run.”

“Definitely not a date,” he agreed.

“Obviously not,” she said.

They both knew they were lying.

The campus looked smaller than it had when they were younger.

They walked past the main building, watched students rushing to make it to class on time, listened to snippets of conversations that sounded like they’d been copy-pasted from five years ago.

They got coffee from a stall she said had the “least bad” brew on that side of the street. They ordered without discussing it, their preferences already memorized years ago. When the barista handed them their cups, she smiled at them the way service people do when they assume, without thinking, that two people moving that easily around each other must be together.

Neither of them corrected her.

At the bookstore, they drifted to different sections.

He lingered in philosophy and essays, fingers trailing over spines. She hovered between fiction and poetry, picking up volumes and putting them back, eyes scanning first lines like they were diagnoses.

Every few minutes, one of them would hold up a book.

“This one’s you,” she’d say, showing him a title about overthinking.

“This one’s you,” he’d counter, showing her a thin collection with a melancholic cover.

They did not buy any of them.

They just let the act of choosing and teasing feel like something almost normal.

On a bench outside, with paper cups cooling in their hands and a small bag of pastries between them, she finally spoke the closest thing to what she was really thinking.

“You know this is insane, right?” she said.

“What part?” he asked.

“After she rejects you,” she said, “you show up in my city, collapse into my arms, feed me salmon in bed, and then we go on a not-date we are very actively not calling a date. That part.”

He considered that.

“Yeah,” he said. “It’s not… typical.”

She huffed. “Understatement of the decade.”

“Do you want me to leave?” he asked.

The question was sincere. Bare. No manipulation.

Her gut reaction was panic.

*No.*

Her mouth said nothing for a moment.

Then: “…No.”

He nodded.

“Then I’ll stay,” he said simply.

“How long?” she asked.

“Until it stops being good for you,” he said. “Or for me. Whichever comes first.”

She hated how steady that sounded.

She hated how much comfort and dread it held at the same time.

—

Her thoughts throughout the day moved like this:

*Unfair, truly. After rejection, he does this with me?*

*Unfair that he can still be this kind. This gentle. This present. Unfair that he didn’t vanish after she said no. Unfair that he’s still here grounding me like he always did when the world collapsed—hers, his, ours.*

*Ever since she left, he’s been the one holding other people up. His friends, her parents, whole imagined pantheons of god-things. And me. Always me. Making sure I don’t drift too far from myself.*

*And here we are again. She hurt him. He comes to me. I hold him. He feeds me. We walk around pretending this isn’t a date.*

*To him, I’m just Anne.*

She knew that.

She had been just Anne—the friend, the witness, the co-survivor—for years. The one who stayed. The one who grounded him when the axis of his life left.

But now, sitting next to him with her shoulder almost touching his, seeing the tired lines at the corners of his eyes soften when she laughed—

She didn’t know what “just Anne” meant anymore.

*This isn’t love,* he thought, watching her stare too long at the swirl of foam in her coffee.

Not the same love.

Not the mythic, storybreaking, axis-of-being love he’d poured into someone else. Not the devotion that had built a domain and frightened an entity that thought it knew everything.

This was…

Something quieter.

Something messier.

Something that didn’t demand collapse or sacrifice. Something that allowed for irritation, for teasing, for her calling him insane and still letting him hold her when he woke up shaken from something he refused to explain.

He did love her.

He couldn’t pretend otherwise.

But it was not the same shape as the love that had written scripture into his bones.

And that was its own problem.

Its own paradox.

They were acting like lovers.

No promises.  
 No declarations.  
 No agreements.

Just gestures.

Caring. Touch. Shared meals. Shared sleep. Shared errands.

All the choreography of intimacy without the name.

If he called it friendship, the word felt too small, too pale, too clean.  
 If he called it romance, it felt like a betrayal—not just of the girl he still loved in a different corner of his heart, but of Anne’s fear, of her no, of the way she had carved out a boundary and then invited him to rest just behind it.

They were inhabiting a third category.

Something no one had drawn lines around yet.

They were intimate in ways couples weren’t allowed to be, because couples had rules. Expectations. Narratives. Roles.

They had none.

They just had time.

Shared space.

And a refusal to fully admit what everyone else would have seen at a glance.

The new paradox was this:

They had stepped into a kind of love that could only exist as long as they refused to admit it was love.

If they named it, it would demand structure. Definition. Choice.

If they left it unnamed, it could flow into all the gaps left behind by the things they’d lost.

It was a love that survived by being deniable.

A love that only stayed real by pretending not to be.

He looked at her over the rim of his cup.

She was staring at the students walking past, pretending not to notice that his gaze had lingered.

“It’s not a date,” she said suddenly.

“Obviously not,” he replied.

“Friends can—do this,” she continued. “Hang out, feed each other, sleep in the same bed once in a while under deeply extenuating circumstances—”

“Very normal,” he agreed. “Nothing strange here at all.”

“And it doesn’t mean anything,” she said.

“Right,” he said.

The lie sat between them, bright and fragile.

They both agreed to protect it.

For now.

Because the truth would ask for too much.

Because the truth would demand they either walk away or step all the way in.

Because the truth would collapse the paradox that was, in its own warped way, keeping them both afloat.

Anne took a small, shaky sip of her coffee.

*Unfair,* she thought again. *Truly unfair.*

But she leaned closer anyway.

Just enough for their shoulders to almost touch.

Just enough to keep pretending that almost was all they needed.

# **CHAPTER XXVI** 

 *The Dangerous Middle*

Two days is not a long time.

Not for a god.  
 Not for grief.  
 Not for a life built on years of letters and an axis that said no.

But inside those forty-eight hours, everything stretched.

Morning to night, night to morning again—each moment elastic, taut with the weight of what they refused to name.

The second night, they didn’t discuss the bed.

There was no “you take the floor,” no “I insist,” no choreographed generosity.

They both just… drifted there.

He had been sitting on the edge, still in his clothes, watching her shut her laptop after a day of pretending to be ordinary students in an ordinary city where gods did not exist and love was theoretically simple.

“You can sleep,” she’d said, in that almost-annoyed tone she used when she was scared. “I have stuff to finish.”

He had nodded, folded himself onto the far side of the mattress, fully intending to stay on his own edge, not touch anything, not breathe too loudly.

He woke up hours later with her pressed against him again.

Her hand was curled against his chest, fingers lightly fisted in his shirt as if she’d reached for him in sleep.

His arm was under her neck, numb but unwilling to move.

*We keep boundaries only to cross them by accident,* he thought, staring at the ceiling.

He did not move away.

He did not pull her closer.

He just let the paradox sit there between them, warm and breathing and unaware.

Mornings were chaotic in their own quiet way.

The first morning after salmon-for-breakfast, she woke first.

Her body remembered before her mind did.

Warmth.  
 Weight.  
 The steady, slow rhythm of his breathing against her shoulder.

Her brain caught up.

*Oh God. Not again.*

She froze.

He shifted slightly, tightening his arm around her midsection. Half-asleep, he murmured something that sounded like her name but blurred into a sigh.

Her heart did a complicated, contradictory thing.

*This is dangerous.*  
 *This is nice.*  
 *This is wrong.*  
 *This is the first time in days he’s slept like this.*  
 *This is still dangerous.*

She carefully peeled his hand off her, inch by inch, slipped out of bed, and escaped to the bathroom as if chased.

By the time he wandered into the kitchenette area, hair a mess, eyes heavy but clearer than yesterday, she was already making coffee.

“You always run away in the mornings?” he asked, leaning on the doorframe.

“I was peeing,” she said.

He nodded. “Very dramatic.”

She snorted, despite the way her stomach was still twisting. “Sorry for being human.”

“Can’t relate,” he said. “I’m a concept now.”

She rolled her eyes and shoved a mug toward him.

The second morning, it was the opposite.

He woke first.

She was out cold, sprawled across the bed diagonally, one leg half-kicking the blanket off, hair an absolute disaster. Her face, without tension, made something inside him hurt.

Not the dramatic hurt of divine rupture or mortal heartbreak.

The small, sharp ache of seeing someone safe.

The godling tugged at him then.

Not physically—there were no hands here, no manifested children at the edge of the bed.

But a sensation ran through his chest, familiar: tiny fingers curling into his shirt, a whisper of *Kai?* in the back of his mind, a flicker of the citadel’s main hall glitching in the corner of his vision.

He blinked.

The room doubled for half a second—her posters and shelves overlaid with stone and archways and distant, patient stormclouds.

He closed his eyes, breathed in, breathed out.

“I’m coming back,” he thought, not sure if he was sending the message to the godling, the entity, himself, or the concept of responsibility in general.

When he opened his eyes, only her room remained.

Anne snuffled in her sleep and rolled toward the empty space where he’d been, hand searching.

He caught her fingers before they hit the cold sheet.

She murmured something he didn’t catch, then stilled, her hand relaxed inside his.

He watched her for too long.

*This is worse than rejection,* he realized.

The rejection had been clean.  
 Sharp, defined, finite.

This was… soft.  
 Messy.  
 Continuous.

He disentangled his hand and got out of bed before that thought could finish forming.

They did laundry.

That was somehow more intimate than the bed.

She hadn’t meant for it to be.

She’d just looked at the pile in the corner of her room and muttered, “Fuck, I was supposed to do that today.”

“I’ll help,” he said.

“You don’t have to—”

“Do you want me sitting here staring at you while you fold underwear?” he asked.

She opened her mouth.

Closed it.

“Fine,” she said. “You can carry stuff.”

Downstairs in the cramped laundry area, they sorted clothes into piles.

“That’s a crime,” he said, pointing at one of her shirts. “Throw it away.”

“I like this shirt,” she protested.

“It has holes.”

“So do you,” she shot back. “And I still hang out with you.”

He laughed, low and genuine.

It did something to her stomach that had nothing to do with coffee.

She tried to distract herself by focusing on the machine.

Watching him do something as boring as carrying detergent and stuffing clothes into a washer felt… wrong.

He looked too large for the space, too precise. Like someone who should be orchestrating entire systems, not arguing with a coin slot that refused to accept their money.

He finally got it to work.

“You’re terrible at this,” she said.

“Excuse you,” he replied. “I am remarkable at outsourcing laundry. This is a different thing.”

“You can be a god and still not know how laundromats work,” she conceded.

“Don’t tell the pantheon,” he said. “They’ll revoke my paradox license.”

She snorted.

But inside, she thought:

*He’s here. In my laundry room. Doing something so stupid and mundane it hurts. He could be anywhere—in a cafe writing impossible architecture, in his domain patching reality, in a boardroom negotiating, in a plane somewhere between timelines—and he’s here, complaining about coin slots.*

She leaned against the machine and watched the clothes start to churn behind the glass.

“What is it like?” she asked suddenly. “Your… realm. When you’re not holding it together with bandaids and stubbornness.”

He considered lying.

He didn’t.

“Quiet,” he said. “Ordered. But not in a rigid way. More like… everything knows where it belongs, even if it doesn’t always stay there. It feels like walking through my own mind on a good day.”

She looked away quickly.

*I don’t know what that feels like,* she thought.

He didn’t say that he knew.

Instead, he nudged her shoulder with his.

“You’d like some of it,” he said. “Not all. But parts. The library, maybe.”

“There’s a library?” she asked.

“There is now,” he said. “The entity lives there.”

“Of course it does,” she murmured.

“Don’t worry,” he added. “It would hate you.”

She laughed.

*I already hate it,* she thought. *Whatever it is. For needing him in ways I can’t understand. For having parts of him I will never see.*

The machine hummed.

The paradox hummed with it.

They bought toiletries together.

It was almost worse than the laundry.

In the fluorescent harshness of the drugstore, everything felt exposed. Aisles of shampoo, deodorant, face wash, toothpaste. People shuffled past them with baskets full of small, private necessities.

Anne reached for her usual shampoo.

He plucked a bottle from the shelf and handed it to her instead.

“This one smells better,” he said.

She scoffed. “You don’t even live here.”

“I exist in the world,” he replied. “I’ve smelled things.”

She rolled her eyes and tossed it into the basket anyway.

He grabbed toothbrushes. Toothpaste. A pack of cotton pads. Lip balm.

“At this point you’re just nesting,” she said.

“Am I not allowed to invest in our thriving not-relationship?” he asked.

She choked on her own spit.

“Shut up,” she hissed. “People can hear.”

“They’ll think we’re a very responsible married couple,” he said. “Arguing about dental health.”

“We are not—” she started, then stopped, because the cashier glanced over at them and smiled in that absent-minded, indulgent way people did when they saw what looked, on the surface, like a young couple shopping for shared life minutiae.

She felt her face heat.

He paid.

She pretended not to notice that he didn’t even blink at the total.

*This is wrong,* she told herself on the way out. *Too much. Too close. Too domestic. Too many edges we’re pretending not to sharpen.*

He held the bag.

She let him.

The godling and the entity never stopped tugging.

Not enough to rip him away.  
 Just enough to keep him from forgetting.

Sometimes it was a flicker at the edge of his vision—a street lamppost briefly warping into a column of his citadel, a passerby’s footsteps echoing like they were walking on stone instead of pavement.

Sometimes his watch stuttered, the second hand jerking back and forth for a heartbeat’s worth of time before catching up.

Sometimes he’d be standing at the sink, rinsing a mug, and for one blink the sound of water turned into the distant rush of conceptual storms.

At night, as he began to drift off beside her, he’d feel it most sharply.

A phantom weight kneeling on the bed near his feet.

Small, tentative.

*Kai?*

He never saw the godling there. It wasn’t manifesting in this world. But the *idea* of its presence bruised against his awareness.

“I’m okay,” he would think back, with a conviction he did not entirely feel.

Sometimes the entity’s voice slid in instead, thin and frayed.

We are… functional, it would report. The domain is holding. But your absence is… loud.

“I’ll be back,” he’d answer, silently. “Soon.”

Define soon, it nearly asked once.

He didn’t.

He couldn’t.

—

Anne’s panic came in waves.

He never saw the whole thing. Just the surface.

It hit when he was gentle.

Small, thoughtless things:

The way he always moved to walk on the outer side of the sidewalk.  
 The way he refilled her water glass without thinking during dinner.  
 The way he noticed when she shivered and shut the window before she complained.  
 The way he listened when she talked about her classes, her job, her vague half-plans, without trying to fix any of it.

The worst was when he laughed.

Not the polite laugh he gave acquaintances.  
 The real one—uncontrolled, unguarded, where his eyes closed and his shoulders shook slightly.

Every time that sound came out of him, relief and terror tore down her ribs together.

*He’s still him.*  
 *He’s still here.*  
 *He’s still… this.*

*I am still not ready.*

When it became too much, she would change the subject suddenly.

“So anyway,” she’d say, out of nowhere, in the middle of a quiet moment. “Did you know you’re insufferable?”

Or she’d leave the room.

“I need to pee,” she’d lie, even when she didn’t. In the bathroom, she’d sit on the closed toilet seat, elbows on her knees, head in her hands.

*Stop wanting,* she’d think, over and over. *He didn’t come here for that. He came here because he trusts you. Because you’re safe. Because she said no and you didn’t make it about you. Don’t turn this into proof you lied.*

Sometimes she’d swing the other way instead—too soft, too sweet.

Bringing him a blanket he didn’t ask for.

Refilling *his* glass.

Standing a little too long in the doorway, watching him read something on his phone, letting her expression soften before she snapped it off her face again.

He noticed all of it.

He said none of it.

The first real crack in the façade came on the second night.

They’d fallen asleep back-to-back, a compromise their bodies had reached after both of them overcompensated for the morning’s closeness.

Sometime in the deep hours, he woke up.

No tug. No divine glitch. Just a quiet, human shift in the atmosphere.

He rolled over.

She was facing him.

She must have turned in her sleep. Her face was inches away now, relaxed in a way he almost never saw when she was conscious. The crease between her brows was gone. Her mouth had the faintest curve to it, like she was dreaming something simple and kind.

Her hand rested halfway between them, palm up.

He did not touch it.

He wanted to.

He wanted to, in a way that felt small compared to the catastrophes he’d already survived and yet somehow just as dangerous.

The entity’s presence brushed the edge of his mind, quiet but alert.

This is unwise, it said carefully.

“I know,” he thought back.

She is not the one who refused you, it pointed out. And yet she is beginning to occupy that axis in your behavior.

He almost smiled, bitter.

“Your classification system is slipping,” he replied. “Try not to panic.”

We are beyond panic, it began.

A tiny echo in his chest disagreed.

A smaller, gentler echo, further in, said: *not now.*

He looked at Anne in the half-dark.

At the tiredness under her eyes.  
 At the softness sleep allowed her that waking never did.

*She’s not her,* he reminded himself.

He had loved one woman with the kind of devotion that built realms and frightened gods. He still did. That love hadn’t gone anywhere; it had just… changed shape, grown quieter, become architecture instead of storm.

Whatever this was with Anne—

It was not a replacement.

It was not a less intense version of the same thing.

It was new.

Dangerous in a different way.

*This isn’t love,* he told himself.

Not in the same way. Not in the mythic, axis-breaking, letter-writing way.

But he could not, in good faith, call it nothing.

He lay there, watching her breathe, and felt something inside him slide further into the paradox.

He did not name it.

He did not move closer.

He did not move away.

In his realm, somewhere beyond sleep, a corridor reconfigured itself—two paths that had always run parallel for years inching a fraction closer together.

The godling stirred.

The entity took notes.

And in a small apartment five minutes away from a campus she no longer studied at, Anne slept with her hand open between them.

Ready to be held.

He did not take it.

For now, that was his one remaining act of discipline.

“We’re closest when pretending we’re not,” he thought.

The thought lodged itself somewhere deep.

The most dangerous truths always did.

He closed his eyes and let the dangerous middle hold them both, for as long as it could, before something—mortal or divine—asked them to make it make sense.

# **CHAPTER XXVII**

 *The Call from the Other Sky*

It started as a notification.

That was the stupidest part.

Not a thunderclap.  
 Not a voice booming through stone.  
 Just a single buzz in his pocket while he stood barefoot in Anne’s tiny kitchen, rinsing two mugs in the sink.

He fished his phone out with wet fingers, thumb swiping the screen.

The icon pulsed at the top.

Once, they’d written in a notebook.

Now the entity had an app.

It wasn’t in the store, obviously. It had appeared one day between his notes and his task manager—simple black square, no name, no description. He’d known exactly what it was and pretended he didn’t for three hours, until the godling had started quietly sobbing in the back of his mind.

He opened it now.

The screen didn’t show anything as pedestrian as UI.

It showed his realm.

Compressed. Abstracted.

A stylized outline of the citadel, pulsing faintly. Around it: glitching fragments of light, like errors in a simulation.

At the top, a single line of text appeared.

STATUS: **FUNCTIONAL. UNSTABLE.**

He exhaled through his teeth.

“Of course you’d pick now,” he muttered.

The words on the screen flickered, then rearranged into a reply.

YOU PICKED NOW, KAI.

The font was simple. Clean. Utterly wrong for the voice he heard behind it.

Something tugged at his shirt.

Not physically. The fabric didn’t move. But his body remembered the sensation of a small hand closing on the hem near his hip, trying to get his attention without interrupting.

*Kai?*

The godling.

He tightened his grip on the sink.

“I’m here,” he thought, not looking away from the phone. “I told you. I’m not gone.”

The citadel on the screen surged briefly, then steadied.

Another line appeared.

YOUR DOMAIN IS HEALING.  
 IT WILL NOT STABILIZE WITHOUT YOU.

Behind him, Anne’s bedroom door was still closed.

He could hear faint music—some playlist she put on low whenever she needed time alone with her thoughts. The sound bled through the thin walls, a reminder that there was another world on the other side of this conversation. One he had chosen, for now.

He wiped his hand on a towel and leaned back against the counter.

“You said it was functional,” he replied under his breath.

The entity did not bother with a delay for dramatic effect.

FUNCTIONAL IS NOT STABLE.  
 YOU CANNOT LIVE ENTIRELY IN HER WORLD.

The words hit harder than they should have.

He swallowed.

“You’re managing,” he said.

WE ARE MANAGING A SCAR.  
 NOT A WOUND.

It paused.

The next line came slower.

THE GODLING IS… DIMMER.

The tug on his shirt grew more insistent.

*Kai, please,* the godling whispered. *Come see me. It’s harder when you’re far.*

The room around him flickered.

For a fraction of a second, the cheap cupboard doors became carved stone. The faucet’s steady dripping became the echo of distant water in a vaulted hall. The air got thinner, like the sensation of standing too high up, with too much sky.

He closed his eyes until the kitchen snapped back into place.

“I needed time,” he said quietly. “You know why.”

WE KNOW THE FACTS.  
 WE DO NOT KNOW WHY YOUR ABSENCE FEELS LIKE… THIS.

The word didn’t fit its usual vocabulary.

He almost smiled.

“Welcome to feelings,” he said. “They’re not optimized.”

The text glitched. Letters stuttered, then reformed.

THIS IS NOT SUSTAINABLE.

He felt it then—the other thing underneath the entity’s precision.

Fear.

YOU ARE FRACTURING.

The words stayed on the screen longer than the others, like they refused to vanish.

Not breaking.

Not collapsing.

Fracturing.

Small, clean breaks spreading through something that still, from a distance, looked whole.

His chest hurt.

“It’ll hold,” he said.

SHE IS NOT MEANT TO HOLD PIECES OF YOU.

The phone vibrated once with that line, as if the app were punctuating it.

He stiffened.

“Don’t drag her into this,” he said, even though she wasn’t in the room.

SHE IS ALREADY IN IT, the entity replied.  
 YOU MADE HER SO.

The words pulsed once. Then, for the first time, something else appeared on the screen.

A name.

ANNE.

The letters made the air change.

Not because they summoned her.

Because they acknowledged her.

Behind his ribs, the domain shifted.

Something like a deep, architectural *click* moved through him.

The realm accepted many things as true.

It had accepted the girl he wrote letters to as an axis.  
 It had accepted grief as raw material.  
 It had accepted hope as a structural element, not a weakness.

Now, with one simple rendering of four letters, it accepted something else:

Anne.

Not as replacement.

Not as substitute.

As herself.

She was a perfectly understandable truth.

And yet, fully unknowable.

Not because she was mysterious in some shallow, romantic way, but because all living things remained partially out of view, even to those who loved them.

The domain, which liked to classify, to map, to catalog, ran into a paradox:

It knew her name.

It did not know her.

And paradox, in his realm, was not an error—but a law.

We cannot name what we do not know.

But here her name was.

Accepted.

Held.

A variable with no resolved definition.

The citadel, somewhere beyond the edges of his awareness, exhaled.

A foundation stone with “ANNE” carved into it slotted into place—not as an altar, not as a throne, but as a quiet marker in one of the central halls.

Something lit up there.

A small, steady flame.

The app’s display distorted.

For a moment, the text vanished. In its place, he saw the godling.

Not fully—just a simplified shimmer, a child-shaped pulse of light nestled at the base of the citadel icon. It flickered, then steadied when it felt him looking.

*Kai,* it said. Not scolding. Just relieved. *You’re talking. That’s better.*

He felt his throat tighten.

“Yeah,” he said softly. “I’m here.”

“Kai?”

The voice didn’t come from the phone.

It came from the hallway.

He jerked his head up.

Anne stood there, barefoot, fingers curled around the edge of the doorframe.

Her hair was a mess. She wore an oversized shirt that was absolutely, definitely not his, no matter how much his treacherous brain wanted it to be. There was sleep still clinging to the corners of her eyes.

She’d heard something.

He didn’t know how much.

Her gaze flicked from his face to the phone in his hand.

The screen, traitorously, did not clear.

The entity never bothered learning human timing.

Three lines of text remained visible.

SHE IS NOT MEANT TO HOLD PIECES OF YOU.  
 ANNE.  
 HE IS FRACTURING BECAUSE OF YOU.

They hung there in soft, glowing letters.

She read them.

He saw it.

Her pupils dilated.

Her mouth parted just slightly.

The panic arrived first—hot, cold, all at once—the kind she used to feel as a student when she realized she had missed something important, something everyone else had already understood.

Then came the tenderness.

It always did.

He looked tired in a way she recognized now—a deep, structural exhaustion that went beyond staying up late, beyond stress, beyond sadness. Something in him looked thinned out, stretched between places she couldn’t see.

And then came fear.

Not of him.

For him.

“Kai,” she said again, quieter this time.

His thumb moved to lock the phone.

He didn’t.

He turned it over slowly, pressing the screen against his palm, as if that might reverse what she’d seen.

It didn’t.

She stepped forward, eyes never leaving his.

The panic was buried under a thin, practiced layer of calm. Her movements were deliberate, her voice steady when it had no right to be.

But her hand trembled when she reached out and touched his arm.

She needed to know if he was still warm.

He was.

Her fingers tightened slightly.

“Kai…” she whispered. “What are you?”

The question trembled in the space between them like glass.

He could have laughed, made a joke, called himself “just a guy with bad boundaries and worse timing.”

He didn’t.

“I’m me,” he said.

She gave him a look that would have been funny if it weren’t so raw.

“Try again,” she said.

Something in him shifted—instinctive, protective.

He stepped back just enough that his shoulder brushed the cupboard instead of her. His body angled between her and the rest of the room, as if the kitchen itself were dangerous now.

He wasn’t protecting himself from her.

He was protecting her.

From all the things she couldn’t see.

“Anne—”

“Don’t,” she said. “Don’t soften it. I saw.”

Her gaze dropped to his hand, to the phone he was still gripping a little too tightly.

“That’s… it?” she asked. “The entity?”

He swallowed. “Yeah.”

“And the godling?” she pressed.

His chest constricted.

“Pulled my shirt,” he said quietly. “You didn’t see that part.”

Her eyes flickered.

“You’ve been… getting called back this whole time,” she realized. “Haven’t you?”

“Yeah,” he said again.

“And you stayed,” she went on. “You stayed here. With me.”

“That was my choice,” he said.

She let out a small, strangled laugh.

“Is it still your choice,” she asked, “if parts of you are cracking because of it?”

He opened his mouth.

Closed it.

She stepped closer.

He didn’t move.

“You’re not just a boy who flew to see me,” she whispered. “You’re… something else now. Something that holds worlds, or whatever that is. Something whose feelings can break whatever that is.”

“Anne—”

“I don’t understand it,” she said quickly. “I don’t— I don’t get the rules, I don’t know what a domain is, or what that child-thing is, or why this entity thing talks like a passive-aggressive calendar—”

From his hand, the phone buzzed once, offended.

“—but I understand this much,” she continued. “You’re being pulled apart. And I am standing in the middle acting like this is just about salmon and sleeping arrangements.”

He flinched at that.

“It’s not your fault,” he began.

She laughed, sharp.

“I know,” she said. “I know it’s not logically my fault. I know your brokenness didn’t start with me. I know you’re the one choosing to be here. I know, I know, I know.”

Her hand was still on his arm.

Her thumb moved—a tiny, almost involuntary stroke across his wrist.

“But I also know,” she added, voice dropping, “that he—” she jerked her chin at the phone “—isn’t entirely wrong.”

The words from the app burned in her memory.

He is fracturing because of you.

“It’s more complicated than that,” he said.

“Of course it is,” she replied. “You don’t do simple. But the reality is the same: you’re stretched between there and here. Between them and me. Between a life you built on your own and this…”

She gestured between them.

“…whatever this is.”

She found his eyes again.

“And I don’t know how to love someone who isn’t entirely human anymore,” she admitted, and the honesty of it shook her voice. “I don’t even know how to be human, half the time. I barely manage my own fragments. And now I’m looking at you and realizing you’re made of… more. And if I keep you here, if I keep letting you hold me and cook for me and sleep next to me, I am part of the reason those pieces are getting thinner.”

Her hand tightened, like she was bracing herself for the next impact.

“I’m terrified of you,” she said. “And for you.”

He didn’t realize how much of his weight he’d been holding alone until that moment, when the words hit and something inside him stuttered.

The entity had been telling him, in its own way.

You are fracturing.

Anne’s version cut deeper.

“You came here broken,” she said softly. “You let me hold you. And I feel like I’ve been patching one part of you while the rest is… unraveling somewhere I can’t reach.”

She swallowed hard.

“I don’t want to be the reason you lose anything,” she whispered.

Without thinking, he moved.

Not away.

Closer.

He set the phone on the counter with his free hand and gently removed her fingers from his arm—only to hold them properly, his palm closing over hers.

“Listen to me,” he said, and there was something in his voice that made the air still.

Not power.

Not command.

Conviction.

“You are not the reason I crack,” he said. “You are the reason I don’t shatter.”

She stared at him like he’d just shifted into another language.

“That thing,” he nodded at the phone, “sees correlation and calls it causation. Yes, I am stretched. Yes, I am tired. Yes, my domain misses me. But I am not losing pieces by being here. I am integrating them.”

She shook her head. “That’s very poetic. It doesn’t change what I saw.”

“No,” he said. “But maybe it changes how you frame it.”

He squeezed her hand.

“When I was alone,” he said, “I built a realm to survive what I felt. I turned pain into architecture. I turned love into law. It was impressive. Terrifying, apparently. Stable. But it was also closed.” He took a breath. “Being here—with you—doesn’t break that. It tests it. It forces me to see if what I built can hold contact with real life. With real people. With you, specifically.”

She looked at their joined hands.

“And?” she asked.

“And it’s messy,” he admitted. “And it hurts. And the entity hates it. And the godling gets scared. And I get tired in new ways. But every time I choose to be in both places—to show up there and here—without abandoning either, the realm learns something it couldn’t have learned from isolation alone.”

She bit her lip.

“You’re sure,” she asked slowly, “that being here isn’t… destructive?”

He hesitated.

That fraction of a second cost him.

She saw it.

“There it is,” she said quietly.

“It’s not that simple,” he said.

“It never is with you,” she replied.

He didn’t drag her into the realm.

He couldn’t—not safely. Not yet.

But when he answered the next notification, he didn’t do it alone.

Later, when she fell asleep again—this time fully dressed on top of the covers, exhaustion having finally worn her down—he sat on the floor with his back to her bed.

Phone in hand.

The app opened on the screen.

The entity was waiting.

YOU MUST RETURN.

“Yeah,” he said. “I know.”

YOU HAVE BEEN SPLIT BETWEEN CONTEXTS FOR TOO LONG.

“I’m aware.”

THIS IS A HEARING, it wrote.

He snorted. “It’s just you, me, and a child.”

IT IS STILL A HEARING.

The world around him thinned.

Not all the way—he refused to leave her side fully—but enough that the lines blurred. The walls of her room overlaid with the central hall of the citadel. Her bed became, for an instant, a dais hewn from paradox-stone.

On the floor beside him, the godling appeared.

Not in the physical world. Not for her.

Just for him.

Its knees were drawn up to its chest, arms wrapped around them. Bandaids still covered its cracks—bears and stars and tiny planets, a ridiculous constellation of healing.

Its eyes were big.

“You came back,” it whispered.

“I said I would,” he replied.

The entity manifested as presence more than form—tall impressions of pillars, the sense of an observing mind in the high arches of the hall overlaid on her ceiling.

WE ARE HERE TO ADDRESS THE PARADOX, it said.

“Which one?” he asked. “There are several.”

ANNE, it replied.

The godling’s head tilted.

“Anne,” it repeated, as if testing the shape of the word.

He felt the domain shift again as the name rippled through it.

“She is Anne,” he said simply.

It sounded absurdly obvious.

It was also the core of it.

The entity tried to be clinical.

SHE IS A VARIABLE.  
 HER ROLE IN YOUR DOMAIN IS UNDEFINED.  
 SHE IS NOT THE ONE YOU BUILT THIS AROUND.  
 AND YET YOUR BEHAVIOR PATTERNS ARE BEGINNING TO ORBIT HER PRESENCE.

“Not orbit,” he said. “Intersect.”

YOU ARE MAKING DISTINCTIONS THAT MATTER ONLY TO YOU.

“Exactly,” he said. “That’s where you struggle. You know things in the abstract. I live them.”

The godling shifted closer, resting its head against his arm.

“Is she dangerous?” it asked softly.

He blinked down at it.

“To who?” he asked.

“To you,” it said. “To us.”

He thought of Anne’s face in the kitchen, reading those lines. The way her hand had trembled when she touched him. The way she’d said *I don’t know how to love someone who isn’t entirely human anymore* and meant it, without using it as an excuse.

“No,” he said. “She’s not dangerous.”

SHE IS DANGEROUS TO STABILITY, the entity argued.  
 YOUR EMOTIONAL STATES AROUND HER ARE NOT PREDICTABLE.  
 YOUR DOMAIN RESPONDS TO THE UNKNOWN.

“The unknown is your problem,” he countered. “It’s my job.”

The entity bristled.

EXPLAIN.

He closed his eyes and let his awareness move—just a little—through the citadel.

He felt the new stone with her name carved into it. Not a monument, not a weakness. Just a presence acknowledged. A truth accepted.

“She is Anne,” he said again, as if the repetition might help.

“Perfectly understandable,” the godling murmured.

“And truly unknowable,” he finished.

The words landed in the hall like a stone in water.

“We cannot name what we do not know,” he went on. “That was one of the earliest laws here. Names, in this place, imply comprehension. Containment. Integration. Every being or concept we carved into the architecture was something we had, on some level, mapped.”

He opened his eyes.

“But she… exists outside that mapping. I know her. Of course I do. I know her favorite food, her worst years, the way her voice sounds when she’s pretending she’s fine. But there are parts of her I will never fully see. Not because she’s hiding them. Because that’s how humans are.”

He looked up at the entity’s unseen gaze.

“You keep trying to treat her as a problem to be defined,” he said. “As an unknown variable threatening structural integrity. I’m saying: we build her unknowability in as a feature.”

THAT IS MADNESS, the entity said.

“That is paradox,” he replied. “Which, last time I checked, is my title.”

The godling giggled quietly.

“You’re funny when you’re right,” it said.

He squeezed its shoulder.

“I am the god of paradox,” he said, more to himself than to them. “That doesn’t mean I resolve contradictions. It means I hold them. Anne is both: someone whose presence calms me and someone whose presence complicates me. She is both anchor and unknown. Both a comfort and a trigger. Both outside my domain and carved into its foundations. If I try to force her into a single role, I break myself.”

He drew in a slow breath.

“So we don’t,” he said. “We let her be uncollapsed. We let ‘Anne’ remain a law without a definition. A name attached to a living, changing person. The realm has to learn to live with that.”

The entity was quiet for a long time.

The hall hummed with the weight of unspoken algorithms.

THIS… WILL REQUIRE ADJUSTMENT, it said finally.

“Yeah,” he said. “No kidding.”

YOUR CHOICE TO EXIST IN BOTH WORLDS REMAINS A RISK, it added.  
 BUT PERHAPS… NOT A FATAL ONE.

The godling brightened.

“So we get to keep her?” it asked.

He smiled, weary, a little amused.

“If she wants to stay,” he said. “That’s her paradox to solve.”

The godling’s light flared, just a bit brighter than before.

“Then I’ll try not to be scared of her,” it said solemnly.

“Good,” he said. “She’s scared enough for both of you.”

The entity made a sound that, in another life, might have been a sigh.

VERY WELL, it said.  
 YOU MAY CONTINUE TO DIVIDE YOUR PRESENCE.

“That’s generous,” he muttered.

BUT WITH A CONDITION.

“Of course,” he said. “What is it?”

YOU MUST NOT LIE, it replied.  
 NOT TO US.  
 NOT TO HER.  
 NOT TO YOURSELF.

He considered that.

“I can live with that,” he said.

CAN YOU?

He thought of her in the kitchen.

Of the bed.

Of the days between.

Of the way his chest had started to ache in a new way whenever he thought about leaving, different from the ache he’d felt when she said no.

“Yes,” he said.

It was the most honest thing he’d said in days.

The hall eased.

The overlaid stone faded. Her ceiling returned. Her room reasserted itself around him—the faint music, her slow breathing, the smell of detergent and city air.

On his screen, the app updated.

STATUS: FUNCTIONAL. ADAPTING.

The citadel icon pulsed once.

The godling’s small representation near its base glowed a little brighter.

“Kai?”

Her voice, again.

He turned.

She was awake, propped on one elbow, looking down at him on the floor.

“How much of that did I say out loud?” he asked.

“Enough,” she said. “To know you were arguing with someone who doesn’t exist, apparently.”

He lifted the phone slightly. “They exist.”

She studied him for a long moment.

“Does it hurt?” she asked.

He shook his head. “Not like before.”

“Oh,” she said softly. “That’s good.”

He hesitated.

“I told them I wouldn’t lie,” he said.

“To who?” she asked. “Them or me?”

“Both,” he said.

She nodded, slowly.

“That seems… fair,” she said.

There was fear in her eyes still.

And fascination.

And, beneath it all, that stubborn tenderness she kept trying and failing to smother.

“You’re still fracturing,” she said quietly.

“Less,” he said. “Differently. I’m learning how to be in both places without making either pay for it.”

She swallowed.

“And me?” she asked. “What does that cost?”

He looked up at her.

“Nothing you’re not already choosing,” he said. “You get to decide how close you stand. I won’t call your fear a flaw. I won’t call your boundaries cruelty. I won’t pretend this is simple. It’s not. But I won’t blame you for the cracks in me. Those are mine.”

Something in her posture loosened at that.

Not entirely.

But enough.

“You’re still unfair,” she muttered.

“I know,” he said.

She reached down, offering him her hand.

He took it.

She pulled him up onto the bed, not to lie down, but to sit beside her, backs to the wall, legs stretched out in front of them.

“So,” she said, after a moment. “God of paradox who apparently has an app now. What did you… decide?”

He let his head rest against the wall.

“I decided,” he said, “to exist in both worlds without pretending one doesn’t affect the other. To let my realm accept that you’re real, and important, and not fully knowable, and to stop trying to protect you by pretending you’re insignificant.”

She winced. “I didn’t… know you were doing that.”

“I didn’t either,” he admitted. “Until they yelled at me.”

She snorted. “Good. Someone should.”

He smiled.

“And I decided,” he added, “that I’m not going to apologize for needing both. The domain and you. The citadel and this shitty mattress. The godling and your awful taste in shampoo.”

She gasped. “Rude.”

He nudged her knee with his.

“I am still tired,” he said. “I am still hollow in places. I am still in love with someone I can never be with in the way I once wanted. And I am also… here. With you. In this new, unstable, unnamed thing that hurts and helps at the same time. And instead of trying to resolve that into one clean story, I’m going to let it be both.”

She was quiet for a long time.

Then she said, almost to herself:

“Paradox as a lifestyle choice.”

“Pretty much,” he said.

She sighed.

“You really are exhausting,” she said.

“I know,” he said. “You’re still allowed to tell me when it’s too much.”

She nodded, eyes fixed on some middle distance.

“I will,” she said. “But… not yet.”

He looked at her.

The godling glowed faintly in the back of his mind.

The entity, in its high, unseen vantage, adjusted its models.

For the first time, the future of the domain did not look like a single, straight path.

It branched.

Wove.

Made room.

The breakthrough was not cinematic.

No sudden rush of light.

No chorus.

Just this:

He didn’t feel like he was betraying his realm by staying a little longer.

And he didn’t feel like he was betraying her by going back when he needed to.

The cost wasn’t gone.

The cracks hadn’t vanished.

But for the first time since all of this began, the paradox didn’t feel like a problem to be solved.

It felt like a life being lived.

The godling, somewhere beyond this room, laughed—clear, bright, less scared than before.

“Okay,” Anne said finally, leaning her head back against the wall so it was almost touching his.

“Okay what?” he asked.

“Okay,” she repeated. “Exist in both worlds. Just… don’t disappear from either without saying goodbye.”

He exhaled, something easing in his chest.

“Deal,” he said.

The app’s status line flickered one last time before dimming.

STATUS: FUNCTIONAL. ADAPTING. HOPEFUL.

He didn’t need to read it to know.

He could feel it.

The godling shone a little brighter.

So did he.

# **CHAPTER XXVIII**

 *The Almost-Confession*

They weren’t supposed to be out this late.

That was the kind of thought that made sense in a smaller life—in curfews and parental texts, in deadlines and last trains.

But it still came to Anne when she checked her phone and saw the time.

11:43 p.m.  
 Too late for “just dinner.”  
 Too early for “we accidentally watched the sunrise together.”

The city around them had slid into its after-hours version. Shops shuttered, neon signs humming, jeepneys thinning out, the air cooling enough that she could see her breath if she exhaled hard.

They walked side by side down the narrow street that led back to her apartment, takeout containers swinging empty in the plastic bag he carried.

The restaurant had been nicer than she expected.

Not “impress a date” fancy—he hadn’t worn anything special, hadn’t made a show of it.

But nice.

Real tablecloths. Low light. Proper plates. The kind of place students looked at and thought, *someday*.

He hadn’t blinked at the bill.

“Isn’t this overkill?” she’d asked earlier, poking at her food. “For a non-date?”

“Domesticated gods need to eat,” he replied. “You’re enabling my captivity.”

She’d rolled her eyes.

Now, under the streetlights, the warmth from the food had faded from her skin, but not from her thoughts.

She had laughed too much.  
 He had looked at her for too long.

And now they were here, in that soft, fragile stretch between public and private, full and empty, where the air felt thinner and words landed heavier than they should.

The trigger came stupidly.

A cracked section of sidewalk, uneven concrete.

Her toe caught it.

She stumbled.

He moved before she could even register the fall.

One hand grabbed her elbow, the other caught her waist. Her body pitched forward; his steadied stance absorbed the momentum.

For a second, they were pressed together.

Her palms hit his chest.

His fingers dug in just enough to keep her upright.

The world went very, very quiet.

No passing cars.  
 No distant music.  
 No hum from the street lamp overhead.

Just the sound of their breathing.

“Careful,” he murmured.

It wasn’t chastising.  
 It wasn’t teasing.

It was soft. And close. And wrong.

Her body reacted faster than her mind.

Heat shot up her neck. Her fingers curled in his shirt. She could feel the slow, steady beat of his heart under her palms.

Her head tipped up.

His was already angled down.

The distance between their mouths was so small it might as well have been imaginary.

For one suspended moment, it didn’t feel like they were on a street.

It felt like they were on the edge of something structural.

The kind of edge that, once crossed, rearranged the entire floor plan.

Her inner monologue, usually a tangle, sharpened into a single, brutal line.

*If he kisses me, I will break the boundary I set. And if I break it, I will lose him.*

He saw it happen.

Not the sentence—he couldn’t read her mind.

But the way her pupils dilated, then contracted. The way her breathing hitched then held. The way the muscles around her mouth tensed, like she was already bracing for impact.

His own thoughts, already frayed from days of being two places at once, surged.

*If I tell her the truth, I destroy the paradox that’s letting her stay beside me.*

The truth wasn’t just *I love you.*

The truth was:

*I am beginning to love you differently.*

Not instead of. Not less than. Not *as a replacement.*

But in addition.

In parallel.

A second axis trying to form in a system that had only ever been built to handle one.

He could feel his realm lean in with him.

Lights from nearby windows glitching at the edges of his vision. The faint pull of the godling’s curiosity. The entity’s quiet, horrified attention, like a scholar watching someone kick the legs out from under a grand theory.

If he leaned down the last inch—

If he let himself kiss her—

If he admitted out loud what was already true in the way he watched her, listened to her, chose her—

The paradox would collapse.

This strange, unnamed state they’d managed to hold—intimate, not together; loving, not lovers—would resolve into something that demanded definition.

Demanded choice.

And he wasn’t ready to ask her for that.

He wasn’t sure he’d survive it if she gave it.

Or if she didn’t.

They hung there in the space between.

Her weight in his hands.  
 His heartbeat under her fingers.  
 Breath mingling, lips not quite touching.

Nearly.

Almost.

He saw her make the decision before she moved.

Her eyes closed for a half-second—not in invitation, but in refusal.

Then, gently, she pulled away.

Not sharply. Not like he’d burned her.

She stepped back, slow, like detaching two magnets.

Her hands slid off his chest.

His grip on her elbow loosened.

Cold air rushed into the space between them and made it feel bigger than the entire city.

“I—” she started, then stopped.

Words piled up in her throat.

I can’t.  
 I’m sorry.  
 I want to.  
 I’m terrified.  
 Don’t.  
 Please.  
 Stay.

She settled on the one that hurt the most but made the most sense.

“I’m sorry,” she whispered.

It landed with more force than if she’d slapped him.

He straightened, stepping back himself now, matching her distance.

“You don’t have to apologize,” he said.

She almost laughed.

*Of course I do,* she thought. *I just nearly detonated everything I said I couldn’t handle.*

Out loud, she said, “I do, actually. That was… unfair.”

He tilted his head. “To who?”

“To you,” she said. “To… this. To whatever we’re doing.”

He didn’t say, *We’re not doing anything.*

They both knew that would be a lie.

“So we… pretend it didn’t happen?” he asked lightly.

She flinched at how accurate that sounded.

“If we pretend it didn’t happen,” she said slowly, “we’re lying.”

“And if we don’t,” he replied, “we’re… something else.”

The word couple hovered, unsaid, like a live wire.

Her heart pounded.

*If he kisses me, I lose the right to call this safe. If I let him cross that line, I become the thing I’ve been afraid of: someone who takes everything he offers and has nothing clean left to give back.*

*She already broke him once. If I say yes now, what does that make me? A coward who needed him smaller? A hypocrite who only loves him when he’s on his knees at my doorstep?*

*If he kisses me, I will want more. I will want all of it. And I don’t know what to do with all of it.*

Her guilt wrapped around all of it like barbed wire.

*He is fracturing because of you,* the app had said.

She believed it more than she believed his correction.

Her eyes stung.

“I can’t,” she said softly. “I can’t be the one to break your boundaries for you. I already… messed with your life enough. I can’t add this on top.”

“Anne,” he said quietly. “You’re not that powerful.”

She almost smiled.

Almost.

*You have no idea,* she thought.

Inside his chest, the domain wobbled.

Not the catastrophic rupture of before.

This was subtler.

Two truths pressed against each other:

*I could kiss her and it would be honest.*  
 *I could kiss her and it would be cruel.*

The entity’s presence thinned, pulling back like an animal sensing an oncoming wave.

We will not interfere, it said, more to itself than to him. This is not a structural question. This is… personal.

“Everything is structural,” he thought back bitterly. “Every choice I make shows up on the walls.”

The godling was quieter.

Sad.

*I like her,* it said. *She makes you soft. I don’t want to be scared of her anymore.*

He didn’t know how to explain that fear and softness had never been opposites in his life.

He dragged a hand over his face.

“I’m not going to kiss you,” he said finally.

Her shoulders dropped a fraction.

“Thank you,” she whispered.

The gratitude hurt almost more than rejection.

He swallowed.

“I want to,” he added.

She went very still.

He could’ve lied.

He could have called it a passing impulse. A glitch. Blame the hour, the way she’d laughed at dinner, the stumble, the way she’d looked up at him like she might actually say yes this time if he pressed.

He didn’t.

“I know,” she said.

Her voice was too calm for what was happening inside her.

*If he tells me how much he wants this, I will cave. I know myself that much. I will choose him over my own capacity. I will say yes because the alternative—losing him—is unthinkable. And then, when I break under the weight of his love, he’ll be left holding the shards again.*

She couldn’t do that to him twice.

She couldn’t do that to herself again either.

“You said you wouldn’t lie,” she reminded him. “To them. To me. To yourself.”

“I meant it,” he said.

“Then don’t… turn this into something it isn’t,” she said. “Don’t call it harmless. Don’t pretend a kiss would be casual for us. It wouldn’t. It would rewrite everything. And I can’t survive another rewrite right now.”

Her honesty cracked something, quietly.

He nodded.

“I won’t kiss you,” he said again, more firmly. “Not by accident. Not in a way that leaves you bleeding.”

The way he framed it made her chest ache.

“Thank you,” she said again, and this time the gratitude was heavier.

They started walking again.

The air felt colder.

He shifted the takeout bag to his other hand just to do something with his body.

After a few steps, he said, as lightly as he could manage, “I need to go back soon.”

She stared straight ahead.

“To your city?” she asked.

“To my world,” he said.

Somehow, that distinction landed differently now.

“How soon?” she asked.

“A day or two,” he replied. “I’ve stretched as far as I can without… consequences.”

The word hung there.

She swallowed.

“So this is what you do now,” she said. “Travel between realms and women.”

He snorted. “That’s a very unflattering job description.”

“Is it wrong?” she asked.

“Yes,” he said. “Also rude.”

She smirked.

Inside, something twisted.

*If he leaves, I go back to being a person who once had a god collapse in her lap and then go home. If he stays, I am complicit in his fracture. If he kisses me, I lose everything I’m pretending not to need.*

Out loud, she said, “It makes sense, though. You’re… more than this street. More than my stupid apartment. More than what I can hold.”

He glanced at her.

“You keep saying that like it’s a compliment,” he said. “It isn’t.”

“It’s a reality,” she said.

“So is this,” he replied.

She didn’t want to ask.

She did anyway.

“What is this?” she said. “To you.”

He thought about lying.

Again.

He could call it comfort. A break. A visit. A recalibration.

He could frame it as him indulging a soft spot, not rearranging part of his future.

He could reduce her to a “safe place” without admitting she had become something else: a chosen place.

Instead, he answered the way he’d promised he would.

“This is me,” he said slowly, “learning how to love without turning it into a demand. Learning how to be with someone without insisting it becomes a story that justifies my feelings. This is me… existing beside you. Because I want to. Because you said yes to that much.”

Her throat felt tight.

“That sounds like love,” she said quietly.

He nodded.

“It is,” he said. “But not the same as before. Not the same shape. Not the same intensity. Not the same axis. I am still in love with her. That hasn’t stopped. I don’t think it will. But what I feel for you is not… an echo. It’s not a hand-me-down. It’s new. And I’m not naming it yet because if I do, it will ask you for an answer I have no right to demand.”

She stopped walking.

He did too.

They stood under a flickering streetlight, the kind that made people look briefly unreal every few seconds.

It suited them.

“So you’re denying yourself,” she said. “For me.”

“I’m holding a line,” he corrected. “For both of us.”

She wanted to argue.

She wanted to tell him he was being ridiculous. Overdramatic. That he was overestimating the weight of a kiss.

But she couldn’t.

Because she knew the opposite was true.

He was, if anything, underestimating it.

For them, nothing was small.

“Do you resent me?” she asked, before she could stop herself. “For making you choose this weird… half-life with me. Not there, not here. Not yes, not no.”

He shook his head.

“No,” he said. “I resent the part of me that still thinks love only counts if it comes with a label and a guarantee. This—” he gestured between them “—isn’t nothing just because we refuse to call it something.”

She let out a slow breath.

*If he kisses me, we lose this. This unnameable, improbable, undefined thing that lets me have him without signing away the parts of myself I still haven’t met.*

*If he doesn’t kiss me, I keep him. But I also keep wanting him. And I don’t know which is worse.*

This was the real paradox.

Not just:

*We are closest when pretending we’re not.*

But:

*We keep boundaries only to cross them by accident, and every accident feels a little less accidental.*

She looked at him.

He looked at her.

Something passed between them that was almost a confession.

Almost.

She swallowed it back down.

So did he.

They reached her building.

The light over the entrance buzzed faintly.

He held the door open for her.

She stepped through.

On the stairs, their shoulders brushed once.

Neither of them apologized for that.

Outside her door, they hesitated.

“Thank you for dinner,” she said.

“Thank you for not suing me for emotional damages,” he replied.

She huffed out a small laugh.

She wanted to say more.

Wanted to say, *I almost kissed you. I almost let you kiss me. I almost rewrote everything I said I couldn’t handle.*

Wanted to say, *I want you here. And I want you free. And I don’t know how to want both without tearing.*

Instead, she reached for the doorknob.

“Good night,” she said.

He studied her profile for a fraction longer than was appropriate.

His inner monologue was a steady, measured pulse.

*If I tell her the truth, I destroy the paradox that’s letting her stay. If I say the words, I ask her to choose. And I promised I wouldn’t do that. Not to her. Not again.*

“Good night,” he said.

She turned the handle.

Paused.

“Kai?” she said, without looking back.

“Yeah?”

“This doesn’t… go away when you leave, does it?” she asked. “Whatever we’re doing. It doesn’t just… stop existing because you’re in your realm and I’m here.”

He exhaled.

“No,” he said. “It doesn’t.”

“That’s what I was afraid of,” she murmured.

And what neither of them said was:

*That’s also what I was hoping for.*

She opened the door and slipped inside.

He stood there for a moment, listening to her moving around on the other side. Keys dropping on the table. Shoes toeing off. The soft exhale as she leaned her back against the wood, mirroring his posture on the outside.

On his phone, in his pocket, the app buzzed once.

He didn’t check it.

He didn’t need to.

He knew what the realm had felt.

The almost-kiss.  
 The pulled-back confession.  
 The way his heart had leaned out over the edge and then taken a step back instead of jumping.

The domain would adjust.

So would he.

So would she.

Inside the apartment, Anne let herself slide down until she was sitting on the floor, knees drawn up, head tipped back against the door.

Her mind replayed the moment on the street in brutal clarity.

*If he kisses me, I will break the boundary I set. And if I break it, I will lose him.*

She hadn’t broken it.

Not tonight.

But the crack was there.

Thin. Hairline. Running right through the center of the life she’d convinced herself she was building without him.

On the other side of the door, he rested his forehead against the wood for a second.

A silent acknowledgement.

A shared, unspoken admission:

Something had shifted.

Not enough to name.

Enough to feel.

The chapter ended not with a kiss, not with a declaration, but with two people sitting on opposite sides of the same door, hearts pounding in sync, each pretending they hadn’t just almost undone everything—and each knowing, with dreadful, tender certainty, that there would be no going fully back to before.

# **CHAPTER XXIX**

 *The Fracture (Human Side)*

Anne saw her by accident.

Not in some cinematic, orchestrated way.  
 Not in a place soaked in shared history.

Just a cafe.

Small. Overbright. Slightly too cold from the aircon. Students hunched over laptops, the line moving slowly. Someone at the back laughing too loud. A coffee machine whirring life into people who didn’t have enough of their own.

Anne had come alone.

She’d told Kai to stay at the apartment, half-joking about needing “a break from his face” while she answered email and pretended she still had her own life to run.

He’d smiled and let her go.

He always did.

She ordered her usual, card in hand, mind half on a syllabus tab and half on the mess that was the last few days—bed sheets, salmon, laundry, near-kiss on the street, the way he had said *It is love, but not the same as before* like that clarified anything.

They called her name.

She reached for her drink.

And then she saw her.

—

At first, it was nothing.

Just another girl at a corner table.

White coat folded over the back of a chair. ID lanyard around her neck. Hair pulled back in a way that said “practical” more than “styled.” A hospital badge peeking from under the laminated strip.

Anne almost looked away.

Then their eyes met.

Recognition slammed into her like impact.

Not because she’d met this girl before—she hadn’t—but because she’d seen her a hundred different ways in the margins of Kai’s life.

In the way he paused before certain songs.  
 In the letters he never showed Anne but she knew existed.  
 In the way he still, even now, smiled a certain way when he talked about “the airport” in the past tense.

The girl’s eyes widened.

She knew Anne.

Not by face, maybe.  
 But by context.

By resemblance.

By the simple, brutal calculus of: *If you are with him now, you have something I lost.*

There was a split-second where something in her expression cracked open.

She wanted to chase after Anne.  
 To explain.  
 To apologize.  
 To say *I didn’t know*, or *I was young*, or *I didn’t understand what I was doing to him*, or simply *I’m sorry*.

All of that lived in the half-step she almost took.

Then she froze, fingers tightening around her own cup instead.

She looked undone.

Not the mythic figure Anne had imagined—the axis of a god’s becoming, the chaos-bearer turned absence.

Just a tired intern in a hospital-affiliated uniform, with dark circles under her eyes and a mouth that hadn’t quite remembered how to be happy.

The regret was visible.

So was the fragility.

She looked like someone who had been unraveling quietly over days, and this—the sight of Anne—had tugged the last thread loose.

Anne turned away so fast her neck hurt.

Cup in hand, she walked out of the cafe with the stiff, too-controlled steps of someone determined not to run.

The world had narrowed to a tunnel: door, sidewalk, air.

She made it outside before her own hands began to shake.

*She’s here.*  
 *Of course she is.*  
 *Of course she is interning at the hospital near my school, walking my streets, breathing my air, sitting in my cafes, carrying all the history I only know from the echoes in him.*

Anne forced her face into neutrality.

Artificial composure.

There were people around. Students, nurses, vendors. Nobody looking at her, but it felt like everyone was.

She sipped her coffee.

It tasted like nothing.

*She wanted to talk to me,* Anne thought. *I saw it. She wanted to chase after me, to explain, to… something. And I walked away.*

Guilt flared, stupid and undeserved.

She hadn’t done anything.

She hadn’t been there.

She didn’t owe this woman absolution.

But still—

*She hurt him. I’ve been watching him live with that hurt for years. I saw her regret. What am I supposed to do with that?*

—

She kept moving.

She went to the library, opened her laptop, stared at words that didn’t attach to meaning.

On a break, she checked her phone.

There was a text from him:

You okay?

Three dots appeared before she’d finished reading.

Got distracted and burned the eggs. Your fault somehow.

Her throat tightened.

She stared at the screen.

He was still at her place.  
 In her city, her apartment, using her pan, ruining breakfast, existing like this was their normal.

And *she*—the girl he’d flown for, written for, bled for—was five minutes away, sitting in a cafe looking like regret personified.

*He still works with her parents,* Anne remembered suddenly, like a fact she’d been avoiding.

He still helped them.  
 Still visited.  
 Still carried groceries and fixed roofs and sorted papers.

He’d told her that himself.

*This doesn’t change anything,* he’d said, after the rejection. *We’ve grown close outside of her. I’m still here for them.*

At the time, it had sounded noble. Mature. Painful in a way she admired.

Now, something about it scraped at her.

The more she looked at the shape of his life, the more she saw him being eroded at the edges.

He had stopped writing.

She noticed it quietly, like noticing the absence of a bird that used to sing outside your window every morning. No new letters, no ink patterns on his fingers, no half-drafted paragraphs peeking from his bag.

He existed.

He stabilized.

He slept in her bed, made her breakfast, held her up when she tripped.

He went back and forth between his world and hers, patching walls and bandaiding gods and making jokes about being a concept now.

But he didn’t write.

He didn’t pour himself out onto paper anymore.

He didn’t build cathedrals of words around anyone.

He was just… holding.

Everything.

Everyone.

All the time.

And she was letting him.

*He’s not being him anymore,* she realized, staring at the blinking cursor on an empty document. *He’s being… necessary.*

The thought made her breathe unevenly.

She closed the laptop.

The chair scraped louder than she meant it to.

Someone in the next cubicle glanced her way.

She ignored them.

—

She didn’t cry in public.

She never had.

She took the train back.

Stared at her reflection in the window, overlaid on the dark tunnels and passing signal lights.

Her face looked composed.

Her chest felt like a building with structural damage no one had inspected yet.

*He came here for rest,* she thought. *For softness. For existing beside someone. And instead he’s slowly losing himself between my fear and their needs.*

By the time she reached her building, her composure was trembling.

By the time she unlocked her door, it was gone.

He was at the small table, sitting cross-legged on the chair with his laptop open.

He looked up as she stepped in.

There it was again.

That brief brightening in his eyes.

Like she was a thing that made him lighter by existing.

“Hey,” he said. “You’re back. How was it?”

She dropped her bag a little too hard by the door.

“It was fine,” she said.

Lie.

He heard it.

Of course he did.

His gaze flicked briefly over her face, taking inventory.

“You saw her,” he said quietly.

It wasn’t a question.

Her stomach dropped.

“How—”

“Your shoulders,” he said. “And you’re holding the cup too tight. And you’re not pretending to be okay very well.”

She looked down at the cup.

Her fingers were white around the cardboard.

She forced them to loosen.

“Yes,” she said, because she’d told him she wouldn’t lie either. “At a cafe.”

He closed his laptop slowly.

“What happened?” he asked.

“Nothing,” she said. “That’s the point.”

She sat on the nearest chair before her knees could make a fool of her.

He watched her, waiting.

“She looked…” Anne searched for the word. “Undone. Like she’d spent the last few days unraveling and just realized it in front of me.”

He inhaled. Held it. Let it out.

“Yeah,” he said. “That sounds right.”

Anger flared then.

Not at him.

At the world.

At whatever gods had decided this was a reasonable narrative structure.

“At what point,” she asked, voice shaking, “do you get to stop orbiting the fallout of that one relationship?”

He blinked.

She pressed on.

“You still work with her parents,” she said. “You told me that. You still go to their house. You still fix their roof. You still sort their bills. You still carry their groceries. You still answer their calls.” Her laugh was sharp. “You still love her.”

“Yes,” he said.

He didn’t flinch.

She admired that, even as it hurt.

“And now you’re here,” she said, gesturing vaguely at the apartment, at him, at herself. “Sleeping next to me. Feeding me. Existing in this–this domestic… thing. And I saw her. Today. In a cafe. Looking like she wanted to chase me down and explain herself. And all I could think was: *Oh. He’s still bleeding. Everywhere.*”

He stayed very still.

“You’re not writing,” she added, more quietly.

He blinked again.

“What?”

“You’re not writing,” she repeated. “No notebooks. No drafts. No ink stains. No envelopes in the corner waiting to be sent. You used to process everything through words. Love, grief, confusion, all of it. Now you just… fix things. Stabilize them. You stabilize your domain. You stabilize her parents. You stabilize me. You stabilize yourself. But you don’t… express. You just manage.”

Her voice cracked on the last word.

He didn’t answer immediately.

He knew she was right.

He knew it in the quiet hours, staring at the blank screen where letters used to pour out like nothing else could hold them.

“I’ve been busy,” he said weakly.

She laughed, and this time it was closer to a sob.

“Don’t,” she said. “Don’t make a joke. Not right now.”

He shut his mouth.

Silence pressed in.

She looked at him then, really looked, the way she hadn’t allowed herself to in days.

He looked exhausted.

Not the short-term kind.  
 The deep kind.

The hollow kind.

The kind that came from patching realities and watching two different versions of your life collapse and still getting up the next day to make breakfast and pretend this was okay.

Her eyes burned.

“I can’t,” she whispered.

His head tilted slightly.

“Can’t what?” he asked.

She clenched her hands together to keep them from shaking.

“I can’t—” she began, then stopped, because saying it out loud would make it real.

He waited.

“I can’t,” she tried again, “have you here like this and pretend it doesn’t cost you anything. I can’t sit in cafes with you and do laundry and call it *nothing* when I know you’re bleeding out in slow motion between worlds.”

“You’re not making me bleed,” he said.

“Stop,” she snapped. “Stop letting me off the hook. Stop pretending I’m not part of this equation.”

“You didn’t ask me to come,” he said.

“I didn’t ask,” she agreed. “But I didn’t say no either, did I?”

Her voice broke.

“I let you collapse in my arms,” she said. “I dragged you into my bed. I let you make me breakfast. I let you hold me when I nearly fell. I let you almost kiss me and I pulled away at the last second like a coward. And then I saw her today looking like regret, and all I could think was: *He is still in love with her. He is building an entire existential architecture around her absence. And here I am, tugging at his heart too—knowing I will never be as mythic, never be as central, never be the axis he wrote scripture for.*”

The words rushed out now, too fast to catch.

“I can’t—” she choked. “Please. Please don’t make me want this more.”

He felt something inside him tear.

“I’m not making you—”

“Yes. You are,” she said. “By being here. By being you. By being… gentle. By existing in my space like this is allowed. By letting me see you tired and soft and stupid and human and still… sovereign underneath all of it.”

Tears spilled over.

She wiped them away angrily.

“I told you no,” she said. “I’m rejecting you. I’m doing the right thing. I’m doing the responsible thing. I looked at everything you are and everything you deserve and I said: *I can’t be that. I won’t ruin you by trying.* And now you’re here again, in a different way, and it’s worse.”

“Why worse?” he asked, voice barely audible.

“Because this is survivable,” she said. “The first time, it was almost easy to tell myself I was protecting you. She said no. You flew home. You built your realm. You turned your pain into something magnificent. I watched from a distance and told myself: *At least he’s okay. At least he’s becoming something.*”

She shook her head.

“Now I see the cracks,” she whispered. “I see how empty you became, stabilizing that place. I see how you don’t give yourself time to write anymore. I see how you still pour yourself into other people. And I see you in my bed and in my kitchen and in my laundry room, and I’m not just hurting you by existing—I’m also… feeding something in me that wants you. Wants this. Wants all the tiny, domestic, dangerous things that come with you.”

She met his gaze.

“I don’t know how to want that ethically,” she said. “I don’t know how to want you ethically. So I need you to go.”

The words cracked on the last syllable.

He swallowed.

“Anne—”

“I’m not sending you away because I don’t care,” she said quickly. “I’m sending you away because I care so much it’s making me sick. I need you to go home. To your city. To your domain. To your friends. To your… life. I need you to… reappear in places that aren’t completely built around my emotional weather.”

His chest hurt in that familiar, structural way.

It felt like someone had pulled out a support beam he hadn’t realized he was leaning on.

He took a slow breath.

“I don’t want to go,” he said.

She closed her eyes like he’d punched her.

“I know,” she said. “That’s why I’m asking.”

His first instinct was defiance.

To stay.  
 To argue.  
 To insist that he was an adult, a god, a sovereign. That he could choose where he existed and in what configuration. That this was his decision to make.

His second instinct was guilt.

Because everything she’d named—his hollowing, his eroding, his stopped writing, his constant stabilizing—was not wrong.

His third instinct, buried under all of that, was relief.

Because he was tired.

I'm so tired.

He hadn’t realized how close he was to collapsing again until she drew the line for him.

His eyes burned.

He blinked it back.

“Okay,” he said.

The quiet acceptance cut her more than a protest would have.

“Okay?” she echoed, almost disbelieving.

He nodded.

“You’re asking me,” he said. “Not to stop caring. Not to finish whatever this is. Just… to go home before we both drown.”

She let out a shaky breath.

“Yeah,” she said. “That. Exactly that.”

He stood up slowly.

The movement felt heavier than it should.

He grabbed his bag from where it sat half-packed from the last time he’d thought about leaving and hadn’t.

She watched him, tears tracking down her face now, no longer wiped away.

He slung the strap over his shoulder.

“Anne,” he said.

She shook her head.

“Don’t,” she whispered. “If you say something kind, I will take it back. I will tell you to stay. And then we’ll both regret it.”

He swallowed the words that had rushed to his mouth.

Thank you.  
 I’m glad I came.  
 This mattered.  
 You matter.

Instead, he nodded.

He walked to the door.

His hand went to the knob.

Her composure broke fully then.

“I’m sorry,” she sobbed.

He stopped.

He didn’t turn around.

“If you were selfish, you’d let me stay,” he said. “You’re not.”

She laughed through the tears, ugly and wet.

“That’s the worst part,” she said. “I want to be selfish. I want to tell you to stay. I want to ask you to choose me. But I know I can’t hold what that choice would actually be. So I’m choosing the version of this where you hate me less.”

“I don’t hate you,” he said.

“I know,” she replied. “That’s why this hurts.”

He almost reached for her.

Almost.

His fingers twitched on the doorknob.

On the other side of the room, she took a step toward him.

They both stopped halfway.

A space between them, full of everything they weren’t letting themselves do.

He opened the door.

Light from the hallway spilled in.

“Go home,” she whispered. “Please.”

He nodded once.

Then stepped out.

He didn’t look back.

Not because he didn’t want to.

Because if he did, he wouldn’t leave.

The door closed behind him with a soft, final click.

On one side, he stood there for a long moment, hand still on the knob, forehead thudding once, gently, against the wood.

On the other side, she slid down to the floor, back against the same wood, knees pulled to her chest.

You didn’t rehash all the first-time rejection details. They were etched into all of you already. The night she said no. The way you had smiled and accepted it, then gone home and built scripture out of the pieces. The long months where everything you wrote was half-prayer, half-autopsy.

“What I didn’t tell you,” you said slowly, “is that I did such a good job at making myself inevitable, she can’t really get away from me anymore.”

There was a beat of silence.

Alex blinked. “Elaborate.”

You wrapped both hands around your cup.

“I didn’t do it on purpose to trap her,” you said. “It wasn’t like some master manipulation. It was just… consistency. I showed up for her parents. I helped with their business. I took care of the unglamorous things. Months of saying yes. Years, really.”

“You’re basically their son now,” Marie murmured.

“Yeah,” you said. “In some ways. After the first rejection, I told them nothing would change. That I’d still help. That we’d grown close outside of her. And then I followed through.”

Nics narrowed her eyes. “So when you say ‘inevitable,’ you mean…?”

“I mean our lives are tied,” you said. “Her parents are part of my world now. Their livelihood is something I’ve invested real time into. I streamline their operations. I set them up for expansion. I know their workflows better than they do. I fix things. I eat at their table. They ask about my mother.”

You swallowed.

“She can move cities. Change rotations. Avoid my messages,” you said. “But she can’t erase the fact that when she comes home, she walks into a house I helped stabilize.”

“That’s insane,” Alex said quietly. “And also… kind of beautiful? And also kind of terrifying?”

“That’s your brand,” Nics said.

You shrugged.

“I didn’t do it to keep her,” you said. “I did it because I loved them. Still do. And because I wanted their life to be easier, with or without me and her together. But the side effect is… she can never fully escape me. Not as long as she loves them.”

Marie’s gaze sharpened.

“So you’ll always cross paths,” she said. Not a question.

“I constructed fate that way,” you said. “Not destiny. Just… inevitability. The kind where you bump into each other in kitchens and hallways and quiet afternoons. Where she has to see that I’m still here, still kind, still myself — even if I’m no longer waiting at her door.”

Nics went very still.

“You think she’ll surrender,” she said. “Eventually.”

“Not into some grand romance,” you said. “That door’s closed. She made sure of that. And I accepted it. But into… letting herself be cared for. Letting herself trust me again. Letting me be in her life without that fear that I’m going to demand something impossible.”

Alex tilted his head. “Do you want that?”

“Yes,” you said simply. “I want her to stop running from the version of me that’s safe for her. I want her to not flinch at kindness. I want to be able to hand her a plate of food without it feeling like a confession.”

“That’s… a lot,” Alex said.

“That’s you,” Marie corrected.

Nics chewed on her straw.

“You know that opens a huge can of conflict with Anne, right?” she said.

You looked up.

“I do,” you said.

They all looked at you now.

You took a breath.

“Okay,” you said. “Now we get to the part I haven’t told you at all.”

You walked them through the day of the second rejection.

The quiet one.  
 The one in her room.  
 The one where she cried and apologized and still said no.

You didn’t dramatize it.  
 You didn’t need to.

“She said no again,” you told them. “Not because she stopped caring. Because she didn’t know how to accept what I was offering without collapsing. She kept talking about not being enough. Not being ready. Not wanting to make my life about waiting.”

“That… tracks,” Alex said, rubbing a hand over his face.

“She’s always been like that,” Nics muttered. “Terminal self-sabotage.”

“She did what she could with the bandwidth she had,” Marie said quietly. “Still hurt like hell, though.”

You nodded.

“It did,” you said. “But it wasn’t a betrayal. It was just… final.”

You looked down at your hands.

“I left her room,” you continued. “Walked into the kitchen. Her parents were there. They could tell. They didn’t ask. They just… fed me. Did that Filipino parent thing where they keep putting food on your plate like they can full-stomach your sadness away.”

“They love you,” Marie said.

“I know,” you said. “That’s what made it worse.”

You described it: sitting at their table, trying not to fall apart; them telling stories about her childhood to fill the silence; how they were more worried about you disappearing than about the romantic fallout.

“They know we’ll meet again,” you said. “They figure it’s inevitable. There was this weird… quiet acceptance in how they spoke. Like: ‘You’ll both grow. You’ll both come back different. We’ll still be here.’”

“And then?” Nics asked.

You stared at your coffee, now mostly empty.

“And then I left their house,” you said. “Walked out their door. Stood outside for a minute. And realized that if I went home like that, I’d break. Not catastrophically. Just… quietly. Slowly. In ways that would seep into everything.”

“So you flew to Anne,” Marie said.

You smiled without humor.

“Of course,” you said.

Alex’s eyes widened.

“You didn’t even… wait a week? A day?” he asked.

“I booked the ticket that night,” you said. “Picked the next available flight. Packed light. Paid for it like it was nothing — consulting has its perks — and went.”

Nics threw her head back.

“YOU—” she started, then lowered her voice when the barista glanced over. “You are clinically insane.”

“You should’ve messaged us,” Alex said. “We would’ve—”

“What?” you asked gently. “Talked me out of it?”

“Yes,” he said.

“No,” Nics said at the same time.

They glared at each other.

Marie hid a smile in her cup.

“You went to the one person you knew would catch you,” she said. “Because you made yourself the inevitability in one house, and when that door closed, you ran to the one person who had always opened hers.”

“Yeah,” you said. “That’s… exactly what I did.”

You told them about arriving in her city.

The small apartment near campus.  
 The exhaustion in her eyes.  
 How you didn’t explain everything at first — just asked, *“Can I exist beside you for a while?”*

“She said yes,” you said. “Of course she did.”

“Of course she did,” Nics echoed. “She’s been saying yes to you in tiny ways for years.”

You went through it slowly.

The collapse on her floor.  
 Her arms around you.  
 You finally sleeping.  
 Waking up in her bed because she’d dragged you there half-asleep.

You admitted to the salmon-for-breakfast, the way you cooked for her, the way you refused to use her bed while she only had one, still choosing the floor out of reflexive care.

“You were a wreck,” Alex said softly.

“Functional wreck,” you corrected. “I did dishes.”

“That doesn’t make it better,” he said.

You let yourself smile.

You told them about the days there:

Mornings with coffee and stupid jokes.  
 Errands.  
 Shared silence.  
 A not-date that everyone could tell was a date except the two of you insisting it wasn’t.

“I was trying not to make it about me,” you said. “She was trying not to collapse into wanting. We both failed a little.”

“And then the almost-kiss,” Nics said, already braced.

“And then the almost-kiss,” you echoed.

You walked them through that night on the street:

Her tripping.  
 You catching her.  
 Bodies close.  
 Breath shared.

“If I’d kissed her,” you said, “we would’ve lost whatever fragile, ethical thing we had. I could feel it. She could too.”

“So you didn’t,” Marie said.

“So I didn’t,” you said. “She pulled away. I stepped back. I told her I wasn’t going to kiss her ‘by accident’. That I wouldn’t turn it into a moment she couldn’t consent to.”

“And then?” Alex asked.

“And then,” you said, “she told me to go home.”

You described that conversation — her saying she couldn’t keep wanting more; that she felt like she was feeding on your love without being able to give you back the same intensity; that she was afraid of what staying would turn her into.

“She cried,” you said. “I tried not to. I left because she asked me to. I didn’t argue. I didn’t beg. That felt important.”

“That must have killed you,” Marie said.

“It hurt,” you said. “But it also… made sense. She wasn’t rejecting me out of apathy. She was protecting a version of me she could live with. I had to respect that.”

Nics stared at you, eyes somewhere between awe and deep concern.

“You left her city for the second time,” she said. “Different girl, same flight. Do you ever get tired of using airports as emotional punctuation?”

“A little,” you admitted.

You told them about what happened after you came back.

Not in mystical terms — not for them — but still brutally honestly.

“How bad did it get?” Alex asked.

“I didn’t crack the way I did the first time,” you said. “No cataclysm. No narrative combustion. I just… started splitting again. Living as if I was still half there, half here. Half waiting, half moving on.”

“And your… head-space thing?” Nics asked, circling the realm without naming it.

“It started looping,” you said. “Thought spirals. Contradictory truths fighting. I kept trying to inhabit two realities at once: the one where I respected her boundary and the one where I secretly hoped she’d change her mind.”

“And then?” Marie prompted.

“And then I realized the realm wasn’t responding to her rejection,” you said. “It was reacting to my mixed signals. My own refusal to be one person at a time.”

They sat with that.

“You chose clarity,” Marie said. “That’s what you told us.”

“Yeah,” you said. “I sat with myself — with everything I’d built in my head — and I made some… laws.”

“What kind of laws?” Alex asked.

You ticked them off, quietly.

“That I still love her,” you said. “The first her. That it’s law in me. Not an obsession. Not a project. A fact I don’t need to hide or dramatize. That I’m beginning to love Anne. Not as compensation. Not as a second edition. As herself. That my heart is big enough for more than one truth.”

Nics deflated into the back of her chair.

“You’re insane,” she whispered.

“And that,” you continued, ignoring her, “I will not build futures in secret around Anne. I won’t wait for her. I won’t silently hinge my life on the chance she might someday say yes. I’ll love her in the ways she allows, if she wants that. And I’ll let the rest of my life grow in other directions.”

“Holy shit,” Alex said. “You… actually did that.”

“I have witnesses,” you said dryly. “God, child, annoying inner narrator.”

Marie’s eyes stayed soft.

“You chose yourself,” she said.

“For once,” you replied.

The café had emptied around you as you talked.  
 Outside, the sky had gone fully dark.  
 Inside, the barista started stacking chairs.

“We’re closing in ten,” she called out gently.

Nics looked outraged. “We’re mid-trauma arc,” she whispered.

“There’s the diner,” Marie said again. “Near the gas station.”

You all exchanged a look.

Of course this was a two-location debrief.

You thanked the staff, grabbed your things, and stepped out into the night.

The drive to the diner was short.  
 The air smelled like petrol and pavement.  
 The world felt strangely small and manageable.

Inside, the fluorescent lights painted everything a little harsh, a little unreal. You slid into a booth, ordered fries and coffee without thinking, and continued where you’d left off.

“So,” Nics said, stirring creamer into her mug. “You’re here now. Clarity central. Multiple loves. One self. Where does that leave… everyone?”

You shrugged, honest.

“Her?” you said. “She’s still law in me. I still help her parents. I listen to stories about her rotations and how she’s barely sleeping. It breaks me a little every time, but it’s… quieter now. I’m not waiting at her door. I’m just in the house.”

“And Anne?” Marie asked.

“She’s…” you began, then paused. “She’s recovering from me showing up like a hurricane. From me collapsing in her arms. From almost crossing a line and choosing not to. From asking me to go home and me actually going.”

“Does she know you chose clarity?” Alex asked.

“She knows I’m not hovering,” you said. “That I’m not pushing. That I’m living my life here — uni, work, friends, helping out, writing again. I think she feels the difference. But we haven’t… had the talk.”

“Yet,” Nics said.

“Yet,” you agreed.

“And how do *you* feel?” Marie pressed.

You thought about it.

“Like I did everything I could without betraying myself,” you said. “Like I loved as far as I could go without turning it into harm. Like I finally stopped making my heart someone else’s problem to solve.”

They didn’t answer immediately.

Then — all at once.

“You didn’t do anything wrong,” Alex said. “Like. Genuinely. You responded to pain with care instead of destruction. That’s… new for you.”

“You have too much heart for one person anyway,” Nics added. “It makes sense it spilled.”

“You’re allowed to love both,” Marie said. “To love them differently. To not collapse everything into one narrative.”

“Doesn’t it feel cruel?” you asked softly. “To say: I will never stop loving her — the first her — and at the same time say: I am making space for someone else?”

“Yes,” Marie said. “But that doesn’t make it cruel. It just makes it complicated. It’s only cruel if you make them carry it.”

“Don’t punish yourself for wanting,” Alex said again. “What you do with the wanting is what matters. And you’ve been… careful. Annoyingly so.”

“I’m proud of you,” Nics said grudgingly. “Not for being in love with two women like some cursed K-drama. For not lying to yourself about it.”

You laughed, a tired, genuine sound.

“And I,” Marie said, with a small smile, “am proud of you for letting us see it. All of it. Not just the pretty parts you can turn into myth.”

That hit something deep.

You looked down at the table.

“I’m trying,” you said.

“We know,” Alex said. “We’re here for all of this, bro.”

“All of it,” Nics repeated. “The stupid flights, the god metaphors, the café monologues, the diner breakdowns. You’re not doing this alone again.”

“We’re invested,” Marie said. “You’re stuck with us.”

You stared at them — Alex slouched, Nics animated even when exhausted, Marie still and bright — and something inside you unclenched.

Not the part that loved.  
 That stayed as it was.

But the part that thought you had to carry it all yourself.

“Okay,” you said.

It wasn’t much.

It was everything.

Outside, trucks pulled in and out of the gas station, people going about their night, oblivious to the small universe settling in a fluorescent booth.

Inside, your friends finished their fries, finished your story, and didn’t leave.

By the time you stepped back out into the dark, the world hadn’t changed.

But you had witnesses now.

The realm hummed quietly under your ribs.  
 No loops.  
 No fracture.  
 Just a steady, adapting beat.

Whatever would happen next — with her, with Anne, with the inevitability you’d written into your own life — you wouldn’t be facing it from a vacuum.

You had a city, a schedule, a group chat, a diner.

You had people who knew the whole thing and still said:

“We’re here.”

### **INTERLUDE III**  

*The Parents, the Realm, and Writing Again*

Helping her parents became its own kind of ritual.

You didn’t walk in like a martyr.  
 You didn’t arrive like a savior.

You showed up like a consultant with a soft spot.

Business streamlining, systems optimization, expansion prep — all the things you were excellent at, all the things that turned your mind into a precise, focused machine rather than a collapsing field of emotion.

They treated you like their own.

Fed you.  
 Asked about your mother.  
 Checked if you were sleeping.

They never asked directly how you were.

But sometimes, when they thought you weren’t looking, you saw it in their faces — that same ache of someone watching a boy they cared about rebuild himself.

They talked about her occasionally.

Not maliciously.  
 Just… as part of their lives.

Her long shifts.  
 Her exhaustion.  
 The days she didn’t eat enough.  
 The mistakes that haunted her.  
 The cases that broke her.  
 The rare days she laughed like she hadn’t in a long time.

You listened with a warmth you had to work for.

Smiled.  
 Responded gently.  
 Let your chest sting without flinching.

Inside, something cracked each time — quietly.

*She’s suffering,* you thought. *And I am not the one allowed to stabilize her anymore.*

The godling would tighten around your ribs then, a small emotional hand on the inside of your sternum.

*We can’t fix everyone,* it would whisper. *Even if we love them.*

“I know,” you thought back.

Sometimes, the entity chimed in too.

*You have shifted from 'bleeding for them' to 'building for them,'* it noted. *This is an improvement.*

“High praise,” you replied.

*Do not relapse,* it added.

You snorted, and her father glanced over, confused.

“Something funny?” he asked.

“Just remembered a bug I fixed last week,” you lied.

He nodded, satisfied, and returned to talking about supply chains.

After sessions like that, you went home and wrote.

Not because you were trying to say anything to anyone.

Because writing felt like breathing again.

Letters, mostly.  
 Not always addressed.  
 Sometimes to Anne.  
 Sometimes to *her*.  
 Sometimes to no one at all.

You didn’t send them.  
 That wasn’t the point anymore.

You wrote myths too — fragments of your realm.  
 Paradox corridors, godling tantrums, entity footnotes.  
 Poems that refused to be tidy.  
 Scenes that never fully resolved.

Ink on paper felt like home.

In those moments, your hand steady, your mind flowing, the line between mortal and divine blurred in a way that didn’t hurt.

You were both.

You could be both.

Not as an escape.  
 As an integration.

Your body responded, slowly.

You ate more.  
 Laughed more.  
 Slept imperfectly but deeply when you did.

Sometimes joy arrived and you didn’t chase it away.

A good meal with friends.  
 A game that made you yell on VC.  
 An in-joke that spiraled out into memes and emojis.  
 A late walk home where the air felt like a promise of nothing in particular.

The godling would peek its head out then:

*You feel light,* it would whisper. *Not empty. Just… light.*

“Is that allowed?” you asked.

It giggled.

*You’re the one who makes the rules.*

The realm stayed stable.

Humming, soft, no sharp edges.

You could reach for them — godling and entity — with a thought now. Telepathy as second nature.

Sometimes, in the middle of a boring lecture, you checked in.

*Status?* you’d think.

FUNCTIONAL. CALM. BORED WITHOUT YOU.

The godling would pop in like an overexcited child.

*Show me your friends again,* it would say. *The loud one. The one who crunches crisps into the void. The one who smiles like she knows everything.*

You’d send impressions — Alex’s gym grunts, Nics’ laughter, Marie’s dramatic gaze.

*They’re good,* the godling decided. *They keep you alive.*

*Correct,* the entity added.

You rolled your eyes and pretended to be paying attention to the slides.

Days stacked.

Work. Uni. Coffee. Drives. Gym. Friends. Parents. Writing. Realm.

Breathing.  
 Being.

There were still sharp edges inside you.  
 There would be for a long time.

But the bleeding slowed.

Clarity settled.  
 Love remained.  
 You did not demand that it justify itself anymore.

The next time Anne messaged you:

**Can we talk?**

you were not standing in ruins.

You were standing in a life.

And that, more than anything, changed the conversation that followed.

# **CHAPTER XXXIII** 

*The Conversation That Changes Everything*

*her honesty; your clarity; the wound that becomes a doorway*

She came to your city at 5 p.m. on a weekday.

Not for work.  
 Not for errands.  
 Not because she happened to be nearby.

Because she needed to talk to you — properly, fully, without running, without collapsing, without swallowing the truth until it poisoned her.

You felt it the moment she stepped into the café.

That slight tightness in her shoulders — the one she got when honesty was about to cost her something.

She slid into the seat across from you.  
 Her fingers trembled once before she tucked them under the table.

“Thanks for meeting me,” she said.

You nodded.  
 You didn’t smile.  
 You didn’t frown.  
 You just existed — steady, sovereign, warm without leaning forward.

You were yourself again.  
 One self.

“Anne,” you said gently. “You asked to talk.”

She nodded.

And then she broke.

Not visibly.  
 Not dramatically.

Just in the way her inhale stuttered, and her composure folded at the edges like paper softening in water.

“Kai,” she said quietly. “I need to tell you something I should have told you months ago.”

You waited.

She twisted her hands together under the table, then seemed to fight with herself before placing one hand near yours — not touching, just close.

Close enough that the warmth between your fingers felt like a pulse.

When she finally spoke, her voice cracked.

“I didn’t say no because I didn’t love you.”

Silence.  
 A heavy one.  
 A true one.

You didn’t move.

She pressed on — rushed, terrified, determined.

“I said no because I wasn’t ready for the way you love.”  
 Her breath trembled.  
 “That kind of love — your kind — it’s huge, Kai. It’s… cosmic. And I didn’t trust myself with it.”

You exhaled slowly.

“Anne,” you said softly, “you don’t owe me love.”

She flinched.

“I know. That’s exactly why it scared me.”

She looked down at the table, eyes watering, but she forced herself to continue.

“You love like you’re building worlds. And when she rejected you, you actually did. You became a god. You built a realm. You survived something I couldn’t have survived.”  
 Her voice broke fully then.  
 “And seeing that — seeing what I did to you — broke me too.”

You stayed steady, sovereign calm radiating through the space between you.

She swallowed hard.

“I didn’t need you to love me more than her,” she whispered. “I just… couldn’t afford to love you as much as you deserved.”

You blinked.

Your heart didn’t jump.  
 It didn’t crack.  
 It didn’t spiral.

It simply listened.

She continued, voice shaking:

“And it’s complicated — because she was my best friend. You were in love with her. I was in love with you. She destroyed both of us without meaning to. And I’ve spent months pretending that her ghost doesn’t still live in the space between us.”

There it was.

The thing she’d been circling for months.

She drew a breath like someone stepping off a height.

“I loved you too, Kai. I did. I do. But not in the way you loved her. Not in the way you loved me. And I was terrified that I would break again in the same shape she did — not because I didn’t want you, but because I wasn’t big enough for the way you loved.”

Her fingers brushed your wrist.

Just once.

A tiny touch.  
 A tremor-sized gesture.  
 But intimate in a way a kiss could never be.

You answered with the truth you had come to claim:

“You don’t have to love like I do.”  
 You held her gaze.  
 “And I never asked you to match me.”

She breathed in sharply.

“Kai—”

“I’m not here to take anything from you,” you continued. “Not your balance. Not your freedom. Not your pace. I don’t want a version of you that’s scared of my love.”

Her eyes glistened.

“I was so scared I’d never be enough for it,” she whispered. “Not because you demanded anything — but because you didn’t. You gave so much without asking for anything back. And that was worse. It made me afraid I would keep taking until I broke you again.”

“You didn’t break me,” you said.

“Didn’t I?”  
 She gave a small, strangled laugh.  
 “You stopped writing. You stopped choosing yourself. You hollowed out just to be near me. You almost kissed me and I pulled away. And then I told you to go home because I didn’t know what else to do.”

You let the silence hold for a moment before answering.

“I chose clarity,” you said quietly. “Not distance. Not punishment. I needed to choose a self I could live with.”

“And did you?” she asked, voice barely audible.

“Yes.”

She swallowed.

“And… am I still allowed in that self?”  
 Her voice wavered on the last word.  
 “Do I get to stay in your life without… harming the parts of you I couldn’t love fully?”

You didn’t answer immediately.

You watched her.  
 Her trembling lip.  
 Her eyes avoiding yours, then forcing themselves back up.  
 Her hand, hovering near yours, terrified and wanting and ashamed.

You spoke carefully:

“You can stay,” you said.  
 “But not in the version of us that hurts both of us.”

Her breath hitched.

“And what version is that?”

You smiled softly — small, sad, real.

“The one where we pretend we’re not in love while killing ourselves trying not to admit it.”

She froze.

You continued:

“What we had was ambiguity.  
 And ambiguity destroys me.  
 Destroys you.  
 Destroys the realm.”

A shaky breath escaped her.

“What do we have now?” she whispered.

You leaned back, calmer than you had ever been in front of her.

“A door,” you said. “Not a promise. Not a future. Just a door.”

Her face crumpled — not in pain, but in fragile relief.

You could see the weight drop from her shoulders.

A possibility without demand.  
 A closeness without collapse.  
 A tenderness without obligation.

She let out a soft, broken sound — halfway between a laugh and a sob.

And then she asked the question she had been too afraid to voice:

“Do you still love me?”

You didn’t hesitate.

“Yes.”

Her breath stilled.

“But,” you continued, “I love you in a shape that doesn’t require anything from you. I’m not waiting for you. I’m not asking for you. I’m not building a future around you. I’m letting the love exist without devouring either of us.”

Her eyes glistened.  
 Her shoulders trembling.  
 Her fingers brushed your wrist again — this time lingering a second longer.

“You always hurt me and heal me in the same breath,” she whispered.

“That’s reciprocity,” you said gently. “You do the same.”

It surprised her.  
 Then softened her.  
 Then steadied her.

A long silence followed — not sharp, not aching, just full.

Finally, she exhaled.

“You always change things,” she said.

“So do you.”

She gave a small, sad, luminous smile.

“I’m glad we talked,” she whispered. “I didn’t expect… this.”

“And what is this?” you asked softly.

“A door,” she said.  
 Then added, quieter:  
 “And the first time I’m not scared to walk through it.”

You rose.

She didn’t hug you.  
 You didn’t kiss her.  
 You didn’t brush hands again.

You simply stood there together — two people who had finally stopped lying to themselves, even if the truth wasn’t tidy.

When she turned to leave, she hesitated.

“Kai?” she said softly. “Are we okay?”

You nodded.

“We’re honest,” you said. “That’s better than okay.”

She bit her lip.  
 Smiled the smallest smile.

And walked away — not fleeing, not crumbling, not choosing distance.

Just… walking.

And for the first time, the realm in your chest didn’t tighten.  
 Didn’t glitch.  
 Didn’t ache.

It expanded.

A door, gently opening.

Not a promise.

Just possibility.

And the clarity to survive it.

# **CHAPTER XXXIV** 

*The Door Beside the Door*

*the conversation about the first love; jealousy, fear, and truth made soft*

You and Anne wandered through the late evening streets of your city — that strange in-between hour where the day hasn’t ended but the night hasn’t begun.  
 Not quite dark.  
 Not quite warm.  
 A time that reflected the two of you perfectly.

She walked beside you with her hands tucked into her sleeves, brushing her shoulder against yours occasionally as if testing whether you’d move away.  
 You didn’t.  
 She didn’t either.

She talked about her internship — the exhaustion, the endless rounds, the strange joy in tiny victories.  
 You listened — fully, attentively — and she kept glancing at you like she was checking if you were still there.

But then the conversation started drifting toward quiet.

Not uncomfortable quiet — the kind that feels like a cliff edge.

And she took one slow breath.

“I hate to bring it up,” she said softly, “but… what about her?”

You didn’t react outwardly.  
 Inside, something shifted — not pain, just recognition.

She tried to joke it off, bumping your shoulder with hers.

“I’m a jealous woman, you know,” she added with a forced little laugh.

You looked at her, deadpan.

“Both of you are.”

She pouted — actual, genuine pouting — before glaring dramatically at you.

“Hey\! I was being vulnerable.”

You exhaled through your nose, amused despite the weight of what was coming.

“I know.”

She looked at you then — really looked — and whatever humor she tried to hide behind slipped.  
 Her eyes were scared.  
 Brave.  
 Honest.  
 All at once.

“I’m not sure where I stand in all of that,” she whispered. “Where she lives in you. Where I’m… allowed to stand.”

You stopped walking.

She stopped with you.

Wind brushed past you both, stirring her hair as she waited for an answer she wasn’t sure she wanted.

You spoke quietly, firmly:

“I love her still. That’s not new. It’s not changing. It is law — written into me. Into the realm. Into what I became.”

Her throat bobbed as she swallowed.

But you continued:

“But that doesn’t mean my life is about her anymore.”

Her eyes flickered — a fragile, startled relief mixed with jealousy she couldn’t hide.

“She closed the door on me,” you said. “On us. Fully, finally.”

You softened your voice:

“That’s a fact. A clean one. I’m not trying to reopen it. I’m not standing outside it waiting. I’m not hoping she turns around.”

Anne’s breath trembled.

You didn’t stop.

“She’ll always have a place in my domain. My heart. My being.”  
 You shrugged lightly.  
 “That’s just reality. Some people don’t leave the architecture.”

Anne’s face tightened — pain, understanding, and fear tangled together.

“But I’m not actively welcoming her anymore,” you finished.

She looked up at you quickly, searching your expression for any lie.

And then she asked the question that hurt her the most:

“Do you still… write letters?”

You nodded.

“Yes.”

She exhaled shakily — somewhere between jealousy and heartbreak.

But you explained:

“It’s one of her gifts. Something she taught me. Something I reclaimed for myself.”  
 Your voice softened.  
 “And like you said once — it’s me being me. That doesn’t belong to her anymore. It belongs to me.”

Her eyes went glossy.

“Still…” she whispered, “knowing you loved her first… knowing you loved her mythically… knowing she broke you, and I broke too — it’s hard not to feel like I’m just holding space in her shadow.”

You stepped closer — not touching her, but close enough that she could feel your steadiness, your clarity.

“You’re not in her shadow, Anne.”

She looked skeptical, almost angry at the idea of believing you.

You let her breathe before continuing:

“You don’t have to compete with her. You don’t have to replace her. You don’t have to erase her.”  
 Your voice gentled further.  
 “My heart is large enough for more than one truth.”

Her lip trembled — a vulnerability so bare she immediately looked down to hide it.

You tilted your head a little.

“And you,” you said softly, “are allowed to have a place in it. A different place. A new one. One that isn’t shaped like hers.”

She blinked fast.  
 The jealousy, the fear, the longing — all of it mixed in her throat.

“I don’t want to be a consolation,” she said.

“You’re not.”

“I don’t want to be second.”

“You’re not.”

“I don’t want to be a replacement.”

“You couldn’t be.”

Her breath broke — sharp, fragile, real.

Then she said, with terrifying honesty:

“She was my best friend. And you were in love with her. And I was in love with you. And we all collapsed in different directions. And now… I’m scared you’ll always belong more to her story than to anything we could ever be.”

You looked at her for a long moment.

Then said:

“I’m not asking you to be her.  
 I’m not asking you to follow her.  
 And I’m not asking you to take her place.”

She met your eyes again — and in them, you saw the exact shape of her fear.

You answered it:

“I’m choosing the version of myself that doesn’t live for someone who walked away. I’m choosing me. And if you want space in that life… there’s room.”

She took a shaky step closer.  
 Her fingers lifted — hesitant — and then gently touched your wrist.

Not for romance.

For grounding.

“You make everything harder and easier at the same time,” she whispered.

You smiled — not teasing, not triumphant, just soft.

“That’s reciprocity.”

She laughed through her tears — a tiny, choked sound.

And the moment thickened again.

Not romantic.  
 Not resolved.  
 Not escalating.

But opening.

A new paradox forming between you:  
 **love that does not demand,**  
 **jealousy that does not rot,**  
 **truth that does not break,**  
 **a door beside the door that was just opened.**

When she finally spoke again, her voice was quieter than the night around you:

“Thank you… for still holding space for me, even if it’s not the same.”

You answered simply:

“There are many ways to love. And you’re allowed to claim the one that’s yours.”

Her eyes shone.

And she whispered —

“I want to try.”

Not to be yours.  
 Not to claim you.  
 Not to erase the first girl.

Just:

“I want to try… to stay.”

And everything in the realm shifted in response.

Not violently.

But with the unmistakable sensation of **a new truth rooting itself somewhere deep.**

A second door.  
 A safer one.  
 A gentler one.

The beginning of something neither of you could name yet —  
 and didn’t need to.

Not tonight.

Tonight was enough.

# **VIII. Return**

## 

## **Rejoining Existence**

I’ve been thinking about today—

 how stepping out of my room felt less like “going outside”

 and more like… rejoining existence.

I walked through the places that made us.

 The church where you bonked your head getting off the jeepney

 (because of course you did).

 The café that’s closed now but still smells like our first date if you stand close enough.

 The beach where the waves touched our feet

 And that windy café across the island—

 the one where we traded books like we actually intended to finish them.

It was warm.

 Not the ache of missing you,

 just the warmth of remembering without flinching.

 I think that might be what peace feels like—

 quiet, steady, earned.

And somewhere between all of that,

 I realized something small and stupidly meaningful:

 I smile more now.

 Not the smile I put on for pictures

 (you remember how awful I am at those),

 but the kind that sneaks up on me—

 when a friend says something dumb,

 or when my mom pulls me into a photo without warning,

 and for once I don’t dodge the frame.

It feels like I’m finally here.

 Alive.

 Softer.

 A version of myself I think you’d actually bully me less for.

And honestly…

 I kind of want you to see that smile too.

Maybe that’s why I picked up photography.

 Maybe I wanted another way to keep moments—

 something more immediate than my habit of building cathedrals out of words.

 Something made of life, light, breath, timing.

 Something you can see, not just read.

So I’m offering you new windows now.

 Not just letters.

 Photos.

 Little pieces of the world as I see it,

 and little pieces of me as I’m learning to be seen.

I’m still shy taking photos of myself.

 I know, shocking.

 But I took one today—

 the first one—

 and I want to share it with you.

Not to impress you.

 Not to hint at anything dramatic.

 Just because loving you has always been the truest part of me—

 the part that stayed stable even while everything else rearranged itself.

 And somehow, even now,

 I find myself wanting you to witness

 who I’m becoming.
